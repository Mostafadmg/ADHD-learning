# The rules for an online hair-loss prescribing and compounding business in the UK

**Status:** COMPLETE (research 23 November 2026). Sections 1 to 4 were written first; sections 5 to 8, the three
lists, the key facts and the summary were added in a second pass the same day, with a working web search.
An independent fact-check of 30 high-impact claims was done the same day; corrections are marked **[Corrected in
fact-check]** in the text and listed in the "Verification log" at the end.

**Reader:** a UK pharmacist independent prescriber planning an online hair-loss service with personalised compounded
topicals, competing with Hims UK, Voy, Hair + Me, Sons and Numan.

**Rules used in this file**

- Every fact carries a source link. Anything that could not be checked at the source is marked **[unverified]** or left out.
- Regulation numbers are quoted only where the regulation was read at legislation.gov.uk (or in the regulator's own
  document) on 23 November 2026.
- No rule is invented. Where the law is unclear, the text says so. Lines marked **Reading:** are the author's
  interpretation of the sources, not a statement by a regulator.
- This is research, not legal advice. Check with a regulatory solicitor and your indemnity provider before launch.

**Words used**

- **Licensed medicine:** a medicine with a UK marketing authorisation (MA). The MHRA has assessed its quality, safety
  and effectiveness.
- **Unlicensed medicine:** a medicine with no UK marketing authorisation. A **compounded** medicine (made up to a
  prescriber's order) is unlicensed. A **"special"** is an unlicensed medicine made by a licensed specials manufacturer
  to a prescriber's order.
- **Off-label:** a licensed medicine used outside its licence (for example oral minoxidil, licensed for blood
  pressure, used for hair).
- **POM:** prescription-only medicine. **P:** pharmacy medicine. **GSL:** general sale list medicine.
- **HMR 2012:** the Human Medicines Regulations 2012 (SI 2012/1916), the main UK medicines law.
- **MHRA:** Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency. **GPhC:** General Pharmaceutical Council.
  **CQC:** Care Quality Commission. **ASA:** Advertising Standards Authority. **ICO:** Information Commissioner's Office.

## 1. The legal routes to supply an unlicensed compounded medicine

### 1.1 The starting point: two bans, each with exceptions

| The ban | What it says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| No supply of an unauthorised medicine | "A person may not sell or supply, or offer to sell or supply, an unauthorised medicinal product." (regulation 46(1)) | https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/46 |
| No manufacture without a licence | A person may not manufacture or assemble a medicinal product "except in accordance with a licence" (a manufacturer's licence) (regulation 17(1)) | https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/17 |

A compounded finasteride and minoxidil scalp spray is both "manufactured" and "unauthorised", so you need an exception
to both bans. UK law gives three workable routes.

### 1.2 Route A: the pharmacy exemption (make it yourself in your registered pharmacy)

**The law.** Regulation 4(1) of the HMR 2012 says regulations 17(1) and 46 "do not apply where any provision of section
10 of the Medicines Act 1968 so provides."
Source: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/4

Section 10(1) of the Medicines Act 1968 lifts both bans for "anything which is done in a registered pharmacy ... and is
done there by or under the supervision of a pharmacist and consists of (a) preparing or dispensing a medicinal product
in accordance with a prescription given by an appropriate practitioner".
Source: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1968/67/section/10

**What that means in plain words**

- You can make an unlicensed medicine **in a GPhC-registered pharmacy**, **by or under the supervision of a
  pharmacist**, **against a prescription** for a named patient. No manufacturer's licence and no marketing
  authorisation is needed.
- A pharmacist independent prescriber is an "appropriate practitioner" for any prescription-only medicine
  (regulation 214(3)(e)). Source: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/214
- **You can make stock in advance.** Section 10(4)(b) allows "preparing a stock of medicinal products with a view to
  dispensing them" against prescriptions, but only where the supply is "at or from that registered pharmacy or at or
  from any other registered pharmacy forming part of the same retail pharmacy business".
  Source: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1968/67/section/10
- **You cannot wholesale it.** The exemption covers retail supply from your own pharmacy business only. The GPhC puts
  it this way: "If the activity is not covered by the exemptions set out in the law, you will need a Manufacturer's
  Specials (MS) licence" from the MHRA.
  Source: https://assets.pharmacyregulation.org/files/document/guidance_for_registered_pharmacies_preparing_unlicensed_medicines_august_2018_0.pdf
- **Labelling.** A pharmacy-prepared medicine is exempt from the normal packaging and leaflet rules, but the label must
  carry the items in Part 2 of Schedule 26: the patient's name, the name and address of the supplying pharmacy, the
  date of supply and, as the prescriber specifies, the product name, directions and precautions (regulation 4(2) to (5)).
  Sources: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/4 and https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/schedule/26
- **A change is coming.** The Human Medicines (Authorisation by Pharmacists and Supervision by Pharmacy Technicians)
  Order 2025 (SI 2025/1249, made 10 December 2025) will amend section 10 so that, in Great Britain, a registered
  pharmacy technician authorised by a pharmacist can do or supervise this work. legislation.gov.uk still lists these
  section 10 changes as "yet to be applied"; the Order says they start "on such days as the Privy Council may by order
  appoint". **[Corrected in fact-check, 23 Nov 2026]** A start date has been set: the Commencement Order of Council
  (SI 2026/110, made 4 February 2026) makes **10 December 2026** "the appointed day for the coming into force of" the
  2025 Order "in so far as it is not already in force". (The first part, on handing out checked and bagged
  prescriptions, started on 7 January 2026.)
  Sources: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2025/1249/made , https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2026/110/made and
  https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1968/67/section/10

**Is there a "special need" test on Route A?** The words "special needs" do not appear in section 10. MHRA Guidance
Note 14 says its guidance and the regulation 167 exemption do "not apply to ... products prepared in a pharmacy under
section 10 of the 1968 Medicines Act and regulation 4 of the Human Medicines Regulations 2012".
Source: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/645e19f5ad8a03000c38b3bc/The_supply_of_unlicensed_medicinal_products__special_GN14.pdf

But three things bring the same idea back in:

1. **The prescriber's duty.** The MHRA tells prescribers to "be satisfied that an alternative, licensed medicine would
   not meet the patient's needs before prescribing an unlicensed medicine", and names pharmacy-prepared medicines as
   one kind of unlicensed medicine.
   Source: https://www.gov.uk/drug-safety-update/off-label-or-unlicensed-use-of-medicines-prescribers-responsibilities
2. **The pharmacy's duty.** The GPhC says the pharmacy's risk assessment "should include finding out whether equivalent
   relevant licensed products exist and are available."
   Source: https://assets.pharmacyregulation.org/files/document/guidance_for_registered_pharmacies_preparing_unlicensed_medicines_august_2018_0.pdf
3. **The advertising ban.** No unlicensed medicine may be advertised (section 2 below), whichever route made it.

**Reading:** Route A is the route a pharmacist-led start-up can use from day one, but it does not remove the need for
an individual clinical reason, recorded for each patient, why a licensed product will not do.

### 1.3 Route B: order a "special" from a licensed specials manufacturer

**The law.** Regulation 167 lifts the regulation 46 ban for a "special medicinal product" if all of these are true:

| Condition in regulation 167 | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| (1)(a) "supplied in response to an unsolicited order" | The order must come from the prescriber's own clinical decision, not from the maker's promotion. |
| (1)(b) made "in accordance with the specification of a person who is a doctor, dentist, nurse independent prescriber, pharmacist independent prescriber or supplementary prescriber" | A pharmacist independent prescriber can specify a special. |
| (1)(c) "for use by a patient for whose treatment that person is directly responsible in order to fulfil the special needs of that patient" | It is for an individual patient with a special need. |
| Condition A (167(2)) | Supplied to one of those prescribers, or "for use under the supervision of a pharmacist in a registered pharmacy, a hospital or a health centre". |
| Condition B (167(3)) | "no advertisement relating to the medicinal product is published by any person." |
| Conditions C and D (167(4), (5)) | Made under adequate supervision and precautions; written manufacturing records kept and shown to the MHRA on request. |
| Condition E (167(6)) | Made in the UK "by the holder of a manufacturer's licence that relates specifically to the manufacture, assembly or importation of special medicinal products". |

Source: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/167

**Records.** Anyone who sells or supplies a special must keep, "for at least five years", the source and date obtained,
who it was supplied to and when, the quantity, the batch number and details of any suspected adverse reaction; show the
records to the MHRA on request; and report any serious suspected adverse reaction (regulation 170). The MHRA says this
applies to pharmacists and prescribers, not only to manufacturers.
Sources: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/170 and GN14 section 8 (link above)

### 1.4 Route C: hold your own Manufacturer's "Specials" (MS) licence

What the MHRA says in Guidance Note 14 (GN14), section 4:

- "The manufacturer or assembler of 'specials' must hold a Manufacturer's 'Specials' Licence granted by the Licensing
  Authority."
- The site "will be inspected for compliance with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) and the conditions of the licence",
  with "a named quality controller and production manager". A Qualified Person (QP) is not required for release.
- Written records of manufacture and supply "must be kept for five years".
- An MS licence holder "may also be a registered pharmacy" using the section 10 exemption, but the labels and paperwork
  of section 10 products "should not make reference to the Manufacturer's 'Specials' Licence or number".

Source: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/645e19f5ad8a03000c38b3bc/The_supply_of_unlicensed_medicinal_products__special_GN14.pdf
(the guidance page, last updated 29 January 2025: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/supply-unlicensed-medicinal-products-specials)

**Reading:** an MS licence is what lets a compounder supply other pharmacies and clinics. It is a GMP manufacturing
operation, inspected by the MHRA, and every order it fills must still meet regulation 167.

### 1.5 The "special clinical need" test and MHRA Guidance Note 14

What GN14 says, in the MHRA's own words:

| Point | Quote | GN14 paragraph |
|---|---|---|
| The exemption is narrow | "In the interest of public health the exemption is narrowly drawn because these products, unlike licensed medicinal products, may not have been assessed by the Licensing Authority against the criteria of safety, quality and efficacy." | 1.5 |
| Licensed first | "An unlicensed medicinal product should not be supplied where an equivalent licensed medicinal product can meet the special needs of the patient." | 2.2 |
| Who decides | "Responsibility for deciding whether an individual patient has 'special needs' which a licensed product cannot meet should be a matter for the ... prescriber responsible for the patient's care." | 2.2 |
| Examples | "an intolerance or allergy to a particular ingredient, or an inability to ingest solid oral dosage forms. These examples are not exhaustive." | 2.2 |
| What does not count | "The requirement for a 'special need' relates to the special clinical needs of the individual patient. It does not include reasons of cost, convenience or operational needs". | 2.3 |
| Evidence | "MHRA expects that documentary evidence of this special need should be obtained by manufacturers, importers or distributors and that this evidence should be made available on request". It "may take the form of a prescriber's letter". | 2.3 |
| Off-label beats unlicensed | "if a UK licensed product can meet the clinical need, even off-label, it should be used instead of an unlicensed product". | 2.4 and Appendix 2 |
| Liability | "the prescriber's responsibility and potential liability are increased when prescribing off-label." | Appendix 2 |
| The specials maker checks too | Makers "should take reasonable steps to establish that persons supplied satisfy the requirements of regulation 167", for example by the person ordering "making clear that where a licensed alternative is available, why that is not clinically appropriate". | 3.2 |

Source for all rows: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/645e19f5ad8a03000c38b3bc/The_supply_of_unlicensed_medicinal_products__special_GN14.pdf

GN14 also cites a European Court case (Commission v Poland, judgment 29 March 2012) in which the Commission argued the
exemption "does not ... justify a derogation based on financial reasons" (GN14 section 10).

**The MHRA's order of preference** (GN14 Appendix 2, "for guidance only"):

1. A UK licensed product used within its licence.
2. A UK licensed product used off-label.
3. An imported product licensed in its country of origin.
4. A UK-made special from a GMP-inspected specials manufacturer.
5. Least acceptable: a product that is not licensed, or not even classed as a medicine, in its country of origin.

### 1.6 What this means for hair loss, where licensed alternatives exist

Facts:

- Licensed UK treatments for male pattern hair loss exist: finasteride 1 mg tablets and minoxidil 5% scalp products.
  Sources: https://www.medicines.org.uk/emc/product/7068/smpc and https://www.medicines.org.uk/emc/product/5765/smpc
- No licensed topical finasteride was found on the UK medicines compendium on 23 November 2026 (see
  `clinical-men.md` in this folder). So a finasteride scalp spray, alone or mixed with minoxidil, is an unlicensed
  medicine in the UK.
- Oral minoxidil is licensed in the UK, but not for hair loss. The ASA recorded the MHRA's advice that oral minoxidil
  "was a prescription-only medicine and was licensed for the treatment of some types of hypertension only".
  Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/menwell-ltd-a25-1277445-menwell-ltd.html
- Hims & Hers UK told the ASA that "their range of treatments included both licensed prescription products and
  compounded, unlicensed prescription products". The ASA ruled on the advertising; the ruling does not say the supply
  was unlawful. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/hims---hers-uk-ltd-a22-1145016-hims--hers-uk-ltd-1.html

**Reading (the author's analysis, not a regulator's statement):**

| Reason for prescribing the compounded product | How it sits against GN14 |
|---|---|
| Documented allergy or irritation to an ingredient of the licensed minoxidil product (for example propylene glycol) | Close to the MHRA's own example ("intolerance or allergy to a particular ingredient"). Strong. |
| Licensed oral finasteride tried and stopped for side effects, or a recorded clinical reason not to take it | A clinical reason specific to the patient. Defensible if recorded. Note that topical finasteride is still absorbed and carries the same warnings (see `clinical-men.md`). |
| "One bottle is easier than two" | This is convenience. GN14 says convenience does not count. Weak. |
| "It is cheaper for us to make our own" or "it is our house product" | Cost and operational reasons. GN14 says these do not count. |
| A higher minoxidil strength (6% or 7%) because "stronger sells" | No clinical reason for the individual patient. Weak. |
| Every patient is moved to the compounded product by default | This looks like routine supply, not an individual special need. High risk. |

A service that offers the licensed options first, records for each patient why they will not do, and only then
prescribes the compounded product, fits the MHRA's and GPhC's guidance. A service whose default product is a compound
does not obviously fit it, even though such services trade in the UK today. **No MHRA or GPhC enforcement action
specifically about compounded hair-loss medicines was found in this research** (the web-search tool was unavailable for
most of it, so this is "not found", not "does not exist").

## 2. Advertising law

### 2.1 The law in one table

All of these are in Part 14 of the Human Medicines Regulations 2012, read at legislation.gov.uk on 23 November 2026.

| Rule | What the regulation says | Link |
|---|---|---|
| What counts as an advertisement | "anything designed to promote the prescription, supply, sale or use of that product" (regulation 7(1)). It does not include the pack or leaflet, or "reference material and announcements of a factual and informative nature", including price lists, "provided that no product claim is made" (7(3)). | https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/7 |
| **No advertising of unlicensed medicines at all** | "A person may not publish an advertisement in Great Britain for a medicinal product unless" a marketing authorisation (or similar) is in force for it (regulation 279(1)). | https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/279 |
| **No advertising of POMs to the public** | "A person may not publish an advertisement that is likely to lead to the use of a prescription only medicine." (regulation 284(1)) | https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/284 |
| Adverts must match the licence and not mislead | An advert must comply with the summary of product characteristics, encourage "rational use ... objectively and without exaggerating its properties", and not be misleading (regulation 280). | https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/280 |
| No "you don't need a doctor" and no diagnosis by post or online | An advert for a medicine may not state or imply "that a medical consultation ... is unnecessary", and may not offer "to provide a diagnosis or suggest a treatment by post or by means of an electronic communications network" (regulation 286). | https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/286 |
| No guarantees, no "better than", no "no side effects" | An advert may not suggest the effects "are guaranteed", "are better than or equivalent to those of another identifiable treatment", or "are not accompanied by any adverse reaction" (regulation 287(1)). | https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/287 |
| No endorsements | An advert may not refer to a recommendation by "scientists", "health care professionals" or celebrities (regulation 289). | https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/289 |
| No free medicine as a promotion | "A person who carries on a medicines business may not sell or supply a medicinal product for a promotional purpose to a person who is not qualified to prescribe medicinal products." (regulation 293(2)) | https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/293 |
| It is a crime | Breach is an offence: a fine on summary conviction, or on indictment "a fine, to imprisonment for a term not exceeding two years or to both" (regulation 303). | https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/303 |

Regulations 283 to 292 apply to adverts "wholly or mainly directed at members of the public" (regulation 282:
https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/282). "Publish" covers any way of issuing an advert,
including online, and includes "causing or procuring" someone else to publish it (regulation 277:
https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/277).

### 2.2 The MHRA Blue Guide, Appendix 6: the rulebook for online clinics

Appendix 6 is called "Advertising of medicines: Guidance for providers offering medicinal treatment services". It is
written for businesses "which provide services that may lead to the prescription and supply of a prescription only
medicine". This is the single most useful document for the website.

| Topic | What Appendix 6 says |
|---|---|
| The basic idea | Services "may promote the service they provide (e.g. medical consultation for individuals with erectile dysfunction ...)" and may give "a balanced overview of the range of therapeutic options. Such material should not draw attention to specific prescription only medicines". |
| Home page | "The Home page should focus on medical conditions and the service the website provides and should not include any reference to named POMs, including price information". Links, hover text and small print on the home page must not name POMs either. |
| Deeper pages | "Further pages about the condition, which the consumer chooses to access, may contain non-promotional information on specific medicines provided this is presented in the context of a fair overview of the treatment options." |
| Consultation first | "It should be clear that the customer is being offered a medical consultation and that this may or may not lead to the provision of a prescription." |
| Prices | On the home page "only indicative prices for a particular medical condition may be provided". A factual price list may sit on other pages but "should not include product claims or actively encourage viewers to choose a product based on the price." |
| Offers | "Special offers on prices of medicines should not be highlighted on the website ... Free offers are not permitted for any medicine." |
| Buttons | "Icons or other features encouraging the purchase of POMs for example, 'Buy Now', 'Buy XXX', 'Add to Basket', etc. should not be used on websites offering POM treatments." |
| Claims | "All information about medicines should be balanced and factual, for example 'XXX is used to treat ...' rather than 'XXX, the best/fastest/strongest/etc. treatment for ...'." |
| **Unlicensed medicines** | "Unlicensed medicines should not be mentioned, to comply with regulation 279 ... Treatments that involve the use of unlicensed medicines may not be described as 'clinically proven' or similar." |
| Web address | "Website addresses should not name specific POMs in their core URL". |
| Paid search | Sponsored links "may promote the service provided but should not mention specific prescription only medicines." |
| Social media | "Any mention of the availability of a named prescription only medicine from a clinic in social media is likely to be considered to be an advertisement which is prohibited." |
| How the MHRA enforces | "We focus primarily on material on the home pages of clinic websites." It "will not routinely review" deeper pages set "in the context of a balanced overview of treatment options", but acts on complaints. It may refer cases to the ASA and may refer clinics and professionals "to their professional regulators if compliance is not achieved in a timely fashion." |

Source: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6012d8c9d3bf7f05c2040b4e/Appendix_6.pdf
(Blue Guide page, last updated 28 March 2025: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/blue-guide-advertising-and-promoting-medicines)

The main Blue Guide adds (section 4.2) that the ban on advertising unlicensed medicines "does not prevent a factual
answer to an unsolicited question about an unlicensed medicine, or about use of a licensed medicine outside the terms
of its licence ('off label' use). Any activity that appeared to be designed to solicit such questions would be likely
to be considered promotional". Section 9.5 says the penalty is "a fine and/or imprisonment for up to two years in most
cases."
Source: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6012d7f2d3bf7f05b92f6cfc/BG_2020_Brexit_Final_version.pdf

### 2.3 The MHRA's extra advice written for hair-loss services

The MHRA has published three pieces of advice aimed squarely at this market.

**November 2019 (erectile dysfunction and hair-loss services).** An advert with any of these features "is likely to
promote the use of medicines containing sildenafil or finasteride in breach of regulation 284":

- "mention of a specific POM by brand name or active ingredient"
- "general references to 'medicines', 'pills' or 'tablets' etc with the clear implication that this is the outcome of a consultation"
- "general images that appear to represent specific medicines or tablets that would be supplied as a result of a consultation"
- "pictures of patient information leaflets for specific POMs."

Source: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5e020ccb40f0b6656d53de5a/Website_treatment_services_Nov_2019.docx

**February 2021 and December 2021 (services that prescribe unlicensed medicines for hair loss and skin care).** To stay
clear of regulation 279:

- "No efficacy claims or other product claims may be made for an unlicensed medicine, since the product would not have
  been formally assessed by an appropriate regulatory body."
- "Treatments that involve the use of unlicensed medicines may not be described as 'clinically proven' or similar".
- "Depictions of bottles or containers that clearly identify the contents as a medicine or that make medicinal claims
  are not permitted ... We would not object to generic depictions of bottles or containers that may refer to a possible
  range of products such as medicines, cosmetics or food supplements."
- "An advertisement must not refer to ingredient(s) that are classified as prescription-only medicines either by name
  or by that legal category. References to the legal category of 'prescription' product(s)/ingredient(s) or similar are
  likely to identify the product as medicinal by presentation and be seen as an advert for a medicinal product."
- "compliance with the legislation is best achieved by focussing on the service provision offered by the website ...
  and on the professional consultation that will take place with the patient."

Sources: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/advertising-investigations-february-2021 and
https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/advertising-investigations-december-2021

### 2.4 The CAP Code (enforced by the ASA)

The UK Code of Non-broadcast Advertising (the CAP Code) covers websites, paid search, social media, emails and posters.
The rules that matter here, from section 12:

| Rule | Text |
|---|---|
| 12.1 | "Objective claims must be backed by evidence, if relevant consisting of trials conducted on people." Medicinal claims "may be made for a medicinal product that is licensed by the MHRA". |
| 12.2.1 | Adverts for medicines "must not offer to provide a diagnosis or suggest a treatment by correspondence, for instance, by post, by e-mail or by other means of an electronic communications network." |
| 12.11 | "Medicines must have a licence from the MHRA, VMD or under the auspices of the EMA before they are marketed. Marketing communications for medicines must conform with the licence and the product's summary of product characteristics." |
| 12.12 | "Prescription-only medicines or prescription-only medical treatments may not be advertised to the public." |
| 12.18 | "Marketers must not use health professionals or celebrities to endorse medicines." |
| 12.19 | An advert for a medicine "may not claim that its effects are guaranteed, that it is absolutely safe or without side-effects or as good as or better than those of another identifiable product." |
| 12.23 | "Marketers must be able to provide scientific evidence, if relevant consisting of trials conducted on people, for any claim that their product or therapy can prevent baldness or slow it down, arrest or reverse hair loss, stimulate or improve hair growth". |

Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/type/non_broadcast/code_section/12.html

TV and radio adverts fall under the BCAP Code. Its rule 11.23 says: "No advertisement for a medicinal product may claim
its effects are guaranteed. That does not prevent the offering of refunds, if the advertisement does not suggest that
efficacy is guaranteed." (quoted in https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/vir-health-ltd-a22-1154781-vir-health-ltd.html)

Separately, since 6 April 2025 the unfair-trading rules in the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024
ban fake reviews and "drip pricing" outright; breaches can bring penalties "of up to the higher of £300,000 or 10%"
of worldwide turnover.
Source: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/unfair-commercial-practices-cma207/unfair-commercial-practices

### 2.6 What the cases teach

1. **The whole impression counts, not only the words.** Pictures of pills or labelled bottles, "prescription", "medication"
   and a landing page that talks about prescriptions have each been enough (Hims 2023, Voy 2025 and 2026, MHRA 2019 advice).
2. **"We advertise a service, not a product" has been tried and has failed** when the page lets the customer pick a
   named treatment and pay (Hims 2023).
3. **Unlicensed means no claims at all**: no "clinically proven", no efficacy figures, no ingredient names, no labelled
   bottle (MHRA 2020 and 2021; Dermatica 2020).
4. **A refund tied to results is read as a guarantee**, even without the word "guarantee" (Numan 2022 and 2024; Hims 2023).
5. **Existing customers are still "the public".** Marketing emails naming a POM were upheld twice (Numan 2023, Voy 2026).
6. **You answer for affiliates and referral codes** (Voy, February 2026).
7. **Paid search is the commonest trap**: "Buy Oral Minoxidil" (Manual 2025), "clinically proven ... or your money back" (Hims 2023).
8. **Repeat breaches reach the pharmacy's own registration.** The GPhC treated continued POM advertising as a failed
   pharmacy standard and moved to statutory enforcement (Voy Pharmacy, August 2026).
9. **Most MHRA cases end with "advertising amended"**, with the company named in a public list. No prosecution of a
   hair-loss company was found.

**Patterns that keep players inside the rules (where they do):**

1. **Lead with the condition and the service**: "hair loss treatment", "personalised plan", "online consultation".
2. **Name the product by what it is, not what is in it**: "Personalised Hair Loss Spray", "Combination spray", "The Drops".
3. **Put the clinician's decision in every call to action**: "Treatment subject to dermatological assessment", "if deemed suitable", "if approved".
4. **Keep named medicines off the home page** and inside deeper condition pages.
5. **State the unlicensed status plainly once the patient is looking at the product**, with the clinical-need wording.
6. **Tie refunds to satisfaction**, not to hair regrowth.
7. **Show the regulated parties**: pharmacy name, GPhC number, superintendent pharmacist, prescriber type.

**Wording still live on these sites that matches things regulators have already acted on** (the author's observation,
not a regulator's finding): results-based money-back guarantees (Sons, Numan; compare the Numan and Hims rulings);
"clinically proven" or "scientifically proven" next to products that include unlicensed medicines (compare the MHRA's
2020 and 2021 advice); named prescription ingredients with efficacy claims and clinician quotes (Hair + Me ingredients
page, Numan finasteride tile; compare regulation 289 and the MHRA's December 2021 advice); headline price discounts on
plans that include POMs (compare Appendix 6 on special offers and the GPhC's August 2026 finding on Voy). A new entrant
should not copy these.

### 3.2 Distance-selling guidance (February 2025): the four things asked about

**(a) Questionnaire-only prescribing**

- "Some medicines are not suitable to be prescribed by a questionnaire model alone." The listed examples are:
  antimicrobials; "medicines liable to misuse"; medicines with "a higher risk of fatality or serious harm if taken in
  overdose"; "medicines and long-term conditions that require ongoing monitoring or management" (narrow therapeutic
  index medicines; "medicines used for diabetes, asthma, epilepsy, heart conditions and mental health conditions";
  pregnancy prevention programme medicines); medicines needing "a physical examination"; "medicines used for weight
  management"; and "medicines labelled with a black triangle". The list is "not limited to" these.
- For those medicines: "The prescriber does not base prescribing decisions on the information provided in a
  questionnaire alone. Instead, the prescriber independently verifies the information the person provides ... This
  could be through timely two-way communication with the person, accessing the person's clinical records, or
  contacting the person's GP, their regular prescriber, or a third-party provider."
- "If the prescriber chooses not to use two-way communication with the person to support their prescribing decision,
  they should make a record, specific to the individual person's circumstances, justifying their reasons."
- For every medicine, the pharmacy must "decide which method of consultation is best suited", including "whether
  timely two-way communication can be established" and whether "there is a need for a physical examination or other
  assessments".

**Reading:** finasteride, topical minoxidil and oral minoxidil are **not named** in the GPhC list. But the list is
open-ended, and two features of hair-loss prescribing point the same way: the MHRA tells prescribers of finasteride to
"review their medical record, ask patients if they have a history of depression or suicidal ideation and review
patients regularly" (section 5.3 below), and oral minoxidil is a blood-pressure medicine used off-label. The safe
design is a questionnaire **plus** photos, **plus** a way to talk to the prescriber, **plus** a check against records
or the GP where the answers leave doubt. The GPhC applied its verification expectation to Voy's pharmacy, which
dispenses hair-loss treatment among others (section 3.5).

**(b) Identity checks**

- "For sales or supplies of P and POM medicines, make sure pharmacy staff can check that the person receiving pharmacy
  services is who they claim to be by carrying out an identity check appropriate for the medicine being supplied."
- Ways to do it: "an outside credit reference database, or a specific identity-checking service using photo ID
  verification", or "the Identity Verification and Authentication Standard for Digital Health and Care Services".
- Systems should flag "multiple orders to the same address", "a person using multiple accounts", "orders that seem
  to be from different people but are using the same payment details", and "requests that are too large or made too
  often".

**(c) Informing the GP**

- For the listed higher-risk categories: "The person has provided the contact details of their regular prescriber,
  such as their GP, and their consent to contact them about the prescription. The prescriber actively shares all
  relevant information about the prescription with other health professionals involved in the care of the person."
- "If the person does not have a regular prescriber, such as a GP, or if there is no consent to share information,
  the prescriber should then decide whether it is safe to prescribe ... The prescriber should make a clear,
  comprehensive record, at the time they make the decision, setting out their justification for prescribing, or not
  prescribing."
- For all services: "all relevant information should be shared with other healthcare professionals involved in the
  care of the person, for example their GP."

**(d) High-risk medicines and off-label use**

- "When supplying medicines for long-term conditions or conditions that require ongoing monitoring or management,
  the prescriber independently verifies the medical history provided by the person ... In addition, the prescriber
  takes responsibility for making sure the necessary monitoring arrangements are in place before prescribing."
- "The prescriber is working within national prescribing guidelines for the UK and good practice guidance. This would
  include following national patient safety alerts and the relevant guidance on prescribing, such as when using a
  licensed medicine for an unlicensed purpose (known as 'off-label' use)."

This applies equally when there is limited, or no, direct contact with the patient". |

Source: https://assets.pharmacyregulation.org/files/document/guidance_for_registered_pharmacies_preparing_unlicensed_medicines_august_2018_0.pdf

**Reading:** finasteride is a hormone-acting drug that must not be handled by women who are or may be pregnant
(see the finasteride SmPC: https://www.medicines.org.uk/emc/product/7068/smpc). Compounding it from powder is the kind
of "hazardous" preparation for which the GPhC says to take specialist advice on containment and staff protection.

## 4. Is Care Quality Commission registration needed?

**Short answer for England:** a service in which **only pharmacists** assess and prescribe, run from a GPhC-registered
pharmacy, does not need CQC registration for its prescribing. A service in which **doctors or nurses** provide or
supervise the treatment does.

### 4.1 Why

| Point | Source text | Link |
|---|---|---|
| The CQC regulates "regulated activities" in England. | "It is an offence to carry on a regulated activity without being registered." | https://www.cqc.org.uk/guidance-regulation/providers/registration/scope-registration (page updated 26 August 2026) |
| The relevant activity is "Treatment of disease, disorder or injury". | It means "the provision of treatment for a disease, disorder or injury by or under the supervision of (a) a health care professional, or a team which includes a health care professional". | Schedule 1, paragraph 4(1), Health and Social Care Act 2008 (Regulated Activities) Regulations 2014: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2014/2936/schedule/1 |
| **Pharmacists are not on the list.** | "health care professional" means a medical practitioner, dental practitioner, dental hygienist, dental therapist, dental nurse, dental technician, orthodontic therapist, nurse, midwife, biomedical scientist, clinical scientist, operating department practitioner, paramedic, radiographer or (since 13 December 2024) an associate. | Schedule 1, paragraph 4(4), same link |
| The CQC says so in terms. | "The list of professionals in the regulations does not include: ... pharmacists ... If you are one of these professionals and you run a standalone treatment service, you do not need to register for Treatment of disease, disorder or injury." | CQC, Scope of registration (August 2026): https://www.cqc.org.uk/system/files/2026-08/260826-Scope-of-Registration-updated-august-2026-1.1_0.pdf |
| Pharmacy services are also a general exception. | "Services which are provided at or from premises that are a registered pharmacy ... and which are (a) pharmaceutical services ... or (b) services of a kind which, if provided in pursuance of that Act, would be provided as pharmaceutical services". **[Fact-check caution]** This exception covers NHS-type pharmaceutical services (dispensing and the like). Whether a private online prescribing service is "of a kind" that would be an NHS pharmaceutical service was not confirmed; do not rely on it. The firm ground is the row above: pharmacists are not on the list. | Schedule 2, paragraph 8, same Regulations: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2014/2936/schedule/2 |
| One doctor or nurse in the team changes the answer. | "If a multi-disciplinary team includes one of these healthcare professionals ... involved in their professional capacity, then the activity will be within scope and needs to be registered." | CQC, Scope of registration (PDF above) |
| Doctors treating online are in scope. | The exception for doctors in private practice "does not apply ... to treatment provided remotely over the telephone, by video consultation or by email." | CQC, Scope of registration (PDF above) |

### 4.2 Side by side

| | Pharmacist-prescriber-led service from a registered pharmacy | Doctor-led (or nurse-led) online clinic |
|---|---|---|
| Regulator of the premises and supply | GPhC (pharmacy registration and inspection) | GPhC for the dispensing pharmacy |
| Regulator of the prescribing service (England) | GPhC, through the pharmacy standards and the prescriber's own registration. No CQC registration for treatment. | **CQC** registration for "Treatment of disease, disorder or injury", with a registered manager and CQC inspections. |
| Regulator of the individual prescriber | GPhC | GMC (doctors) or NMC (nurses) |
| How competitors are set up | Sons names "our partner pharmacy and independent prescribing service" (https://sons.co.uk/). | Voy's pharmacy "dispenses prescriptions issued by a separate prescribing service which is regulated by the Care Quality Commission" (GPhC report, August 2026, link in 3.5). Numan shows "CQC Regulated" (https://www.numan.com/hair-loss). |

### 4.3 Cautions

- **Using a consultant dermatologist changes things.** If a doctor supervises treatment, or sits in the team that
  delivers it, in a professional capacity, the CQC text above brings the service into scope. A doctor who only writes
  clinical policy is a greyer case. **Reading:** ask the CQC in writing before launch if any doctor or nurse is involved;
  the CQC guidance itself says "Contact us if you are not sure".
- **Scotland.** Since 19 June 2024 the definitions of "independent clinic" and "independent medical agency" include
  services provided by "a registered pharmacist" or "registered pharmacy technician", but both definitions exclude "a
  registered pharmacy within the meaning of section 74 of the Medicines Act 1968". So a Scottish service run from a
  registered pharmacy is outside Healthcare Improvement Scotland registration; one run by pharmacists from somewhere
  that is not a registered pharmacy is inside it.
  Source: section 10F, National Health Service (Scotland) Act 1978: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1978/29/section/10F
- **Wales and Northern Ireland** have their own regulators (Healthcare Inspectorate Wales; the Regulation and Quality
  Improvement Authority), named in the GPhC guidance. Their rules were **not checked** in this research.
- **Not being CQC-registered is not a marketing weakness to hide.** The GPhC requires the website to say "whether the
  prescriber is a doctor or a non-medical independent prescriber – for example a pharmacist" (section 3.2).

## 5. Prescribing standards

### 5.1 Off-label and unlicensed prescribing: what the prescriber takes on

The MHRA's statement of prescribers' responsibilities (Drug Safety Update, first April 2009, republished 11 December
2014) is short and applies to every pharmacist independent prescriber.

| Duty | MHRA wording |
|---|---|
| Unlicensed: licensed first | "be satisfied that an alternative, licensed medicine would not meet the patient's needs" |
| Off-label: better than the licensed option | "be satisfied that such use would better serve the patient's needs than an appropriately licensed alternative" |
| Evidence | "be satisfied that there is a sufficient evidence base and/or experience of using the medicine" |
| Ownership | "take responsibility for prescribing the medicine and for overseeing the patient's care" |
| Information | give "sufficient information about the proposed treatment, including known serious or common adverse reactions, to enable them to make an informed decision"; where evidence is limited, "explain the reasons for prescribing a medicine off-label or prescribing an unlicensed medicine" |
| Records | "record the medicine prescribed and, where common practice is not being followed, the reasons for prescribing this medicine"; "you may wish to record that you have discussed the issue with the patient" |

Source: https://www.gov.uk/drug-safety-update/off-label-or-unlicensed-use-of-medicines-prescribers-responsibilities

The GPhC prescriber guidance (section 3.4) says the same in its own words: sufficient evidence, and "a record of
discussions, especially when prescribing ... is for an unlicensed or 'off-label' medicine". GN14 adds that "the
prescriber's responsibility and potential liability are increased when prescribing off-label" (section 1.5).

**What this means for the three common hair-loss products**

| Product | Legal status in the UK | What the prescriber must be able to show |
|---|---|---|
| Finasteride 1 mg tablets | Licensed POM for male pattern hair loss (https://www.medicines.org.uk/emc/product/7068/smpc) | Normal licensed prescribing, plus the 2024 and 2026 MHRA safety steps (5.3). |
| Oral minoxidil (low dose) | Licensed POM, but only for some types of hypertension; off-label for hair (ASA, Manual 2025: https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/menwell-ltd-a25-1277445-menwell-ltd.html) | Why off-label use better serves this patient than licensed topical minoxidil; evidence base; cardiovascular screening and follow-up recorded. |
| Compounded topical finasteride (alone or with minoxidil) | Unlicensed (section 1.6) | The patient's special clinical need (GN14), the evidence base, and that the patient was told it is unlicensed and what that means. |

### 5.2 Remote prescribing guidance

Two documents set the standard for prescribing at a distance:

1. **GPhC "In practice: Guidance for pharmacist prescribers" (April 2025)** and the **GPhC distance-selling guidance
   (February 2025)**: summarised in sections 3.2 and 3.4. The key lines: no high-risk prescribing "based on online
   questionnaires alone"; "a method of timely two-way communication"; independent verification of the history for the
   listed categories; a recorded justification if you choose not to talk to the patient or cannot contact the GP.
2. **"High level principles for good practice in remote consultations and prescribing"**, agreed by the UK health
   regulators (GPhC, GMC, NMC and others) and published 8 November 2019. The GPhC's summary says professionals should
   recognise and protect vulnerable patients, carry out clinical assessments and medical record checks, and raise
   concerns when safeguards are missing. It is not new guidance; it is "underpinned by existing standards".
   Sources: https://www.pharmacyregulation.org/news/principles-good-practice-issued-protect-patients-online-0 and
   https://www.gmc-uk.org/professional-standards/learning-materials/remote-prescribing-high-level-principles
   (the GMC page blocked automated reading on 23 November 2026, so the ten principles were not quoted here).

The Royal Pharmaceutical Society's "Competency Framework for all Prescribers" (updated 2021) is the profession's
description of good prescribing and is the usual yardstick for scope-of-practice and CPD evidence.
Source: https://www.rpharms.com/resources/frameworks/prescribing-competency-framework/competency-framework

### 5.3 Finasteride: the MHRA safety updates of April 2024 and May 2026

**April 2024 (Drug Safety Update, 29 April 2024).** Prescribers should:

- know that "finasteride has been associated with depression, suicidal thoughts and sexual dysfunction", and that
  sexual dysfunction has "persisted ... even after treatment was stopped";
- "before prescribing finasteride, ask patients if they have a history of depression or suicidal ideation";
- "advise patients to stop finasteride 1mg (Propecia) for male pattern hair loss immediately if they develop depression
  or suicidal thoughts and to contact their doctor as soon as possible";
- "monitor patients for psychiatric and sexual side effects";
- note that "a patient card will be introduced in all finasteride packs";
- report suspected reactions on the Yellow Card scheme.

Sources: https://www.gov.uk/drug-safety-update/finasteride-reminder-of-the-risk-psychiatric-side-effects-and-of-sexual-side-effects-which-may-persist-after-discontinuation-of-treatment
and the MHRA press release of the same day, which asks patients to show the card and leaflet to friends and family
because they may not notice mood changes themselves:
https://www.gov.uk/government/news/men-on-finasteride-asked-to-stay-vigilant-for-possible-psychiatric-and-sexual-side-effects

**May 2026 (Drug Safety Update, 11 May 2026): strengthened warnings, now including dutasteride.**

- "finasteride is associated with depression, suicidal ideation and sexual dysfunction which may persist after
  treatment is stopped".
- "inform patients of the risks at point of prescribing" and "advise patients to read the Finasteride patient cards
  and the patient leaflet ... which are both supplied in the 1 mg and 5 mg packs".
- The finasteride 1 mg product information gains a warning "that sexual dysfunction may contribute to mood disorders".
- "when prescribing finasteride, review their medical record", "ask patients if they have a history of depression or
  suicidal ideation" and "review patients regularly for psychiatric and/or sexual side effects".
- Finasteride 1 mg: stop if suicidal thoughts or depression develop, and contact a healthcare professional as soon
  as possible.
- Dutasteride: as a precaution its product information will say "that mood alterations have been reported with the
  same class of medicine (finasteride)"; patients should seek advice as soon as possible if they develop suicidal
  thoughts or depression.
- The UK patient cards introduced in 2024 are kept.

Source: https://www.gov.uk/drug-safety-update/finasteride-and-dutasteride-updated-safety-warnings-for-psychiatric-side-effects-and-sexual-dysfunction

**Reading:** the patient card is in the manufacturer's pack. A compounded topical finasteride has no pack card and no
legal leaflet, and the GPhC expects written information for pharmacy-prepared medicines (section 3.3). The safe
practice is to give every finasteride patient, oral or topical, the same card-style information in writing, ask and
record the depression and suicidal-ideation history, check the medical record where available, and book regular
reviews that ask about mood and sexual function. Dutasteride for hair loss is off-label in the UK
**[licence status for hair loss not re-checked in this file; see `clinical-men.md`]**.

### 5.4 The European review (Article 31 referral, 2024 to 2025)

| Step | Date |
|---|---|
| Review started | 3 October 2024 |
| PRAC (safety committee) recommendation published | 8 May 2025 |
| CMDh position | 19 June 2025 |
| European Commission final decision | 22 August 2025 |

Findings: the EMA "confirms suicidal thoughts as side effect of finasteride tablets", frequency unknown, with most
cases on the 1 mg hair-loss tablet; a patient card goes in the 1 mg pack. For finasteride **skin sprays** it found "no
evidence linking suicidal ideation" to them, so their product information was not changed. For dutasteride it found
"insufficient evidence to establish a causal association" but added a precautionary warning. The benefits of both
medicines "continue to outweigh their risks for all approved uses".
Source: https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/human/referrals/finasteride-dutasteride-containing-medicinal-products

**Reading:** "no evidence linking" is not the same as "safe". The EU sprays reviewed are licensed products; a UK
compounded spray has no such data behind its particular formula. The EMA finding cannot be used in UK marketing for
an unlicensed product (section 2), and it should not be used to skip the mood screening in 5.3.

### 5.5 Consent and record keeping: the minimum a hair-loss record should hold

Pulled together from the sources above (MHRA prescribers' responsibilities; GPhC prescriber guidance; GPhC distance
guidance; GPhC unlicensed-medicines guidance; MHRA finasteride updates):

1. Identity check result and date (GPhC distance guidance).
2. History, including the answers to the finasteride mood questions and any record or GP check made (MHRA 2024 and 2026).
3. Photos or other assessment used, and whether two-way contact took place; if not, why not (GPhC distance guidance).
4. Options discussed, licensed first. For an unlicensed product: the special clinical need and why licensed options
   will not do (GN14). For off-label: why it better serves the patient (MHRA).
5. That the patient was told the product is unlicensed or off-label "and what this means" (GPhC unlicensed guidance),
   and the written information given.
6. GP or regular prescriber details, consent to share, and what was shared; or the recorded justification (GPhC).
7. Monitoring and follow-up plan (GPhC distance guidance: "Include monitoring and follow-up plans").
8. For specials or compounded products: batch number, source and supply details kept "for at least five years"
   (regulation 170 for specials; GPhC batch records for pharmacy preparation).

- The GPhC requires "an identity check appropriate for the medicine being supplied" for P and POM supplies, for

## 7. Indemnity, the responsible pharmacist and the superintendent

### 7.1 Indemnity

- Every pharmacist must have appropriate indemnity cover to register (Pharmacy Order 2010, article 32), defined by
  "the nature and extent of the risks". Cover can be personal, from an employer or through a professional body or
  defence organisation. You must tell the GPhC within seven days if cover lapses. The GPhC "cannot advise" on the
  amount. Source: https://www.pharmacyregulation.org/pharmacists/registering-pharmacist/indemnity-requirements
- The pharmacy itself must have "Appropriate indemnity or insurance arrangements ... in place for the pharmacy
  services provided" (Standards for registered pharmacies, standard 1.5).
  Source: https://assets.pharmacyregulation.org/files/document/standards_for_registered_pharmacies_june_2018_0.pdf
- Prescribers who widen their scope should "check that they are covered by their professional indemnity insurer"
  (GPhC prescriber guidance, section 3.4), and patients must be told the indemnity and regulatory arrangements for
  associated prescribers (GPhC distance guidance, section 3.2).
- **Reading:** tell the insurer in writing that the service (a) prescribes online, (b) prescribes off-label oral
  minoxidil, (c) prepares unlicensed topicals in-house, including finasteride, and (d) supplies by post across the UK.
  Product liability for a pharmacy-made medicine sits with the pharmacy, not a manufacturer, so check the policy covers it.

### 7.2 The responsible pharmacist (RP)

| Rule | Source |
|---|---|
| A retail pharmacy business must have a responsible pharmacist "in charge of the business" at each premises, with a notice displayed showing the RP's name, registration number and that they are in charge. | Medicines Act 1968, sections 70 and 71: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1968/67/section/71 |

### 7.3 The superintendent pharmacist

- A company running a pharmacy needs a superintendent who is a pharmacist **and** "a senior manager of the retail
  pharmacy business who has the authority to make decisions that affect the running" of it; a signed statement goes
  to the registrar; a superintendent who leaves must notify the registrar within 28 days (section 71(6), (7A), (8)).
  Source: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1968/67/section/71
- The GPhC distance guidance makes the owner and the superintendent jointly answerable for meeting it, and names
  "any pharmacists prescribing 'at a distance'" among those it applies to (section 3.1).
- **Change under way.** The Pharmacy (Responsible Pharmacists, Superintendent Pharmacists etc.) Order 2022 lets the
  GPhC set RP rules and professional standards for superintendents and RPs. The GPhC consulted on draft Medicines
  (Pharmacies) (Responsible Pharmacist) Rules 2026 and draft standards for both roles from December 2025; the
  consultation closed on 25 March 2026. The draft moves responsibility for setting and reviewing procedures from the
  RP to the superintendent. Whether final rules have been made was **not confirmed**; until they are, the 2008
  Regulations above apply.
  Source: https://assets.pharmacyregulation.org/files/2025-12/consultation-on-draft-rules-and-standards-for-responsible-pharmacists-and-draft-standards-for-superintendent-pharmacists-December-2025.pdf?VersionId=jYUJed3SMEP3CtiU5Ljng.TTZg3PdOG1

**Reading:** a solo founder can legally be owner, superintendent, RP and prescriber, but the GPhC wants prescribing
kept separate from supply "when possible" with a second competent person doing the clinical and accuracy check
(section 3.4), and the August 2026 Voy inspection shows inspectors test whether the pharmacy really checks the
prescriptions it dispenses. Budget for a second pharmacist from launch.

## You must

- Run the dispensing and any compounding from a GPhC-registered pharmacy, under a pharmacist, against a prescription (Medicines Act 1968 s10).
- Have a superintendent pharmacist (if a company) and a responsible pharmacist signed in at the premises, with SOPs and an RP record kept five years.
- Hold appropriate personal and pharmacy indemnity that names online prescribing, off-label use and in-house compounding.
- Offer and record licensed options first; record each patient's special clinical need before prescribing an unlicensed product (GN14).
- Tell the patient in writing that a product is unlicensed or off-label and what that means.
- Follow the GPhC distance guidance: written risk assessment, appropriate identity checks, two-way contact available, verification where needed, GP sharing or a recorded reason.
- Ask about depression and suicidal ideation before finasteride, give card-style written warnings, review mood and sexual function regularly, and report suspected reactions on the Yellow Card scheme.
- Keep a second competent person checking prescriptions you write where possible, and tell patients they may use another pharmacy.
- Keep batch, ingredient and supply records for compounded products, and a recall system.
- Show on the website the pharmacy's GPhC number, owner, superintendent, the prescriber's name, registration number and profession, and how to complain.
- Pay the ICO fee, do a DPIA, keep a record of processing, use Article 6 plus Article 9(2)(h), host data in the UK or an equivalent jurisdiction.
- Get CQC advice in writing before involving any doctor or nurse in treatment.
- Build subscriptions to the DMCC Act rules (reminders, easy exit, cooling-off) before contracts made from 1 January 2027 (date announced by government on 9 August 2026; regulations still in draft on 23 November 2026).

## You can

- Prepare unlicensed topicals in your own registered pharmacy without a manufacturer's licence, including stock in advance for your own business.
- Prescribe unlicensed and off-label medicines as a pharmacist independent prescriber, within your competence.
- Order specials from an MS-licensed manufacturer instead of making them.
- Run a pharmacist-only prescribing service from a registered pharmacy in England without CQC registration.
- Promote the service and the condition, show indicative prices for the condition on the home page, and give a balanced, non-promotional overview of treatment options on deeper pages.
- Publish a factual price list without product claims (not on the home page).
- Answer a patient's own unsolicited question about an unlicensed or off-label product factually.
- Offer refunds tied to satisfaction, not to results.
- Email existing customers under the soft opt-in, as long as the email does not name or point to a POM.

## You cannot say or do

- Name any POM (finasteride, oral minoxidil, dutasteride) on the home page, in paid search, in social posts, in the web address or in marketing emails.
- Mention, picture or make any claim for an unlicensed (compounded) product in advertising; no labelled bottles.
- Call a treatment that includes an unlicensed medicine "clinically proven" or similar.
- Say "prescription medication" or list prescription ingredients in adverts.
- Use "Buy now", "Add to basket" or "Buy [drug]" buttons for POMs.
- Promise results or refund only if hair does not grow back.
- Say "no side effects", "safe", "best", "strongest" or "better than" another treatment.
- Use doctors, pharmacists, scientists, celebrities or customer testimonials to endorse a medicine.
- Give free medicines or headline discounts on POMs; run "free trials" that are not clearly a free consultation.
- Let affiliates or refer-a-friend users post codes naming POMs (you answer for them).
- Move every patient to the compound by default, or justify it by cost, convenience or "our house product".
- Prescribe higher-risk medicines on a questionnaire alone without verification or a recorded reason.
- Wholesale your pharmacy-made products to other pharmacies or clinics without an MS licence.
- Copy US-style direct marketing of compounded topical finasteride.

## Summary

1. You can legally make compounded hair-loss topicals in your own GPhC-registered pharmacy against your own prescriptions, with no MHRA licence.
2. You cannot sell them to other businesses without a Manufacturer's Specials licence.
3. Each unlicensed prescription needs a recorded individual clinical need; cost, convenience and "house product" do not count, and licensed or off-label options come first.
4. You cannot advertise an unlicensed medicine at all, or a POM to the public: market the condition and the consultation only.
5. Regulators have repeatedly acted against Hims, Numan, Manual/Voy, Sons and others for exactly this; copy their compliant patterns, not their live risks.
6. GPhC distance guidance requires ID checks, two-way contact, verification or a recorded reason, GP sharing, and a visible prescriber identity.
7. A pharmacist-only service from a registered pharmacy in England does not need CQC registration; adding a doctor or nurse to treatment does.
8. For finasteride, ask about depression and suicidal thoughts, give card-style warnings, review regularly and report on Yellow Card; this applies to compounded topicals too.
9. Register with the ICO, do a DPIA, keep health data in the UK or equivalent, and build subscriptions to the DMCC rules expected from 1 January 2027.
10. Get indemnity that names online, off-label and compounding work, plan for a second checking pharmacist, and take legal advice before launch.
