# The rules for an online hair-loss prescribing and compounding business in the UK

**Status:** DRAFT IN PROGRESS (research started 23 November 2026). Sections are filled in as sources are read.

**Reader:** a UK pharmacist independent prescriber planning an online hair-loss service with personalised compounded
topicals, competing with Hims UK, Voy, Hair + Me, Sons and Numan.

**Rules used in this file**

- Every fact carries a source link. Anything that could not be checked at the source is marked **[unverified]** or left out.
- Regulation numbers are quoted only where the regulation was read at legislation.gov.uk (or in the regulator's own
  document) on 23 November 2026.
- No rule is invented. Where the law is unclear, the text says so. Lines marked **Reading:** are the author's
  interpretation of the sources, not a statement by a regulator.
- This is research, not legal advice. Check with a regulatory solicitor and your indemnity provider before launch.

**Words used**

- **Licensed medicine:** a medicine with a UK marketing authorisation (MA). The MHRA has assessed its quality, safety
  and effectiveness.
- **Unlicensed medicine:** a medicine with no UK marketing authorisation. A **compounded** medicine (made up to a
  prescriber's order) is unlicensed. A **"special"** is an unlicensed medicine made by a licensed specials manufacturer
  to a prescriber's order.
- **Off-label:** a licensed medicine used outside its licence (for example oral minoxidil, licensed for blood
  pressure, used for hair).
- **POM:** prescription-only medicine. **P:** pharmacy medicine. **GSL:** general sale list medicine.
- **HMR 2012:** the Human Medicines Regulations 2012 (SI 2012/1916), the main UK medicines law.
- **MHRA:** Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency. **GPhC:** General Pharmaceutical Council.
  **CQC:** Care Quality Commission. **ASA:** Advertising Standards Authority. **ICO:** Information Commissioner's Office.

## 1. The legal routes to supply an unlicensed compounded medicine

### 1.1 The starting point: two bans, each with exceptions

| The ban | What it says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| No supply of an unauthorised medicine | "A person may not sell or supply, or offer to sell or supply, an unauthorised medicinal product." (regulation 46(1)) | https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/46 |
| No manufacture without a licence | A person may not manufacture or assemble a medicinal product "except in accordance with a licence" (a manufacturer's licence) (regulation 17(1)) | https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/17 |

A compounded finasteride and minoxidil scalp spray is both "manufactured" and "unauthorised", so you need an exception
to both bans. UK law gives three workable routes.

### 1.2 Route A: the pharmacy exemption (make it yourself in your registered pharmacy)

**The law.** Regulation 4(1) of the HMR 2012 says regulations 17(1) and 46 "do not apply where any provision of section
10 of the Medicines Act 1968 so provides."
Source: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/4

Section 10(1) of the Medicines Act 1968 lifts both bans for "anything which is done in a registered pharmacy ... and is
done there by or under the supervision of a pharmacist and consists of (a) preparing or dispensing a medicinal product
in accordance with a prescription given by an appropriate practitioner".
Source: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1968/67/section/10

**What that means in plain words**

- You can make an unlicensed medicine **in a GPhC-registered pharmacy**, **by or under the supervision of a
  pharmacist**, **against a prescription** for a named patient. No manufacturer's licence and no marketing
  authorisation is needed.
- A pharmacist independent prescriber is an "appropriate practitioner" for any prescription-only medicine
  (regulation 214(3)(e)). Source: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/214
- **You can make stock in advance.** Section 10(4)(b) allows "preparing a stock of medicinal products with a view to
  dispensing them" against prescriptions, but only where the supply is "at or from that registered pharmacy or at or
  from any other registered pharmacy forming part of the same retail pharmacy business".
  Source: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1968/67/section/10
- **You cannot wholesale it.** The exemption covers retail supply from your own pharmacy business only. The GPhC puts
  it this way: "If the activity is not covered by the exemptions set out in the law, you will need a Manufacturer's
  Specials (MS) licence" from the MHRA.
  Source: https://assets.pharmacyregulation.org/files/document/guidance_for_registered_pharmacies_preparing_unlicensed_medicines_august_2018_0.pdf
- **Labelling.** A pharmacy-prepared medicine is exempt from the normal packaging and leaflet rules, but the label must
  carry the items in Part 2 of Schedule 26: the patient's name, the name and address of the supplying pharmacy, the
  date of supply and, as the prescriber specifies, the product name, directions and precautions (regulation 4(2) to (5)).
  Sources: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/4 and https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/schedule/26
- **A change is coming.** The Human Medicines (Authorisation by Pharmacists and Supervision by Pharmacy Technicians)
  Order 2025 (SI 2025/1249, made 10 December 2025) will amend section 10 so that, in Great Britain, a registered
  pharmacy technician authorised by a pharmacist can do or supervise this work. legislation.gov.uk still lists these
  section 10 changes as "yet to be applied"; the Order says they start "on such days as the Privy Council may by order
  appoint". **Whether a start date has been set was not checked.**
  Sources: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2025/1249/made and https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1968/67/section/10

**Is there a "special need" test on Route A?** The words "special needs" do not appear in section 10. MHRA Guidance
Note 14 says its guidance and the regulation 167 exemption do "not apply to ... products prepared in a pharmacy under
section 10 of the 1968 Medicines Act and regulation 4 of the Human Medicines Regulations 2012".
Source: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/645e19f5ad8a03000c38b3bc/The_supply_of_unlicensed_medicinal_products__special_GN14.pdf

But three things bring the same idea back in:

1. **The prescriber's duty.** The MHRA tells prescribers to "be satisfied that an alternative, licensed medicine would
   not meet the patient's needs before prescribing an unlicensed medicine", and names pharmacy-prepared medicines as
   one kind of unlicensed medicine.
   Source: https://www.gov.uk/drug-safety-update/off-label-or-unlicensed-use-of-medicines-prescribers-responsibilities
2. **The pharmacy's duty.** The GPhC says the pharmacy's risk assessment "should include finding out whether equivalent
   relevant licensed products exist and are available."
   Source: https://assets.pharmacyregulation.org/files/document/guidance_for_registered_pharmacies_preparing_unlicensed_medicines_august_2018_0.pdf
3. **The advertising ban.** No unlicensed medicine may be advertised (section 2 below), whichever route made it.

**Reading:** Route A is the route a pharmacist-led start-up can use from day one, but it does not remove the need for
an individual clinical reason, recorded for each patient, why a licensed product will not do.

### 1.3 Route B: order a "special" from a licensed specials manufacturer

**The law.** Regulation 167 lifts the regulation 46 ban for a "special medicinal product" if all of these are true:

| Condition in regulation 167 | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| (1)(a) "supplied in response to an unsolicited order" | The order must come from the prescriber's own clinical decision, not from the maker's promotion. |
| (1)(b) made "in accordance with the specification of a person who is a doctor, dentist, nurse independent prescriber, pharmacist independent prescriber or supplementary prescriber" | A pharmacist independent prescriber can specify a special. |
| (1)(c) "for use by a patient for whose treatment that person is directly responsible in order to fulfil the special needs of that patient" | It is for an individual patient with a special need. |
| Condition A (167(2)) | Supplied to one of those prescribers, or "for use under the supervision of a pharmacist in a registered pharmacy, a hospital or a health centre". |
| Condition B (167(3)) | "no advertisement relating to the medicinal product is published by any person." |
| Conditions C and D (167(4), (5)) | Made under adequate supervision and precautions; written manufacturing records kept and shown to the MHRA on request. |
| Condition E (167(6)) | Made in the UK "by the holder of a manufacturer's licence that relates specifically to the manufacture, assembly or importation of special medicinal products". |

Source: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/167

**Records.** Anyone who sells or supplies a special must keep, "for at least five years", the source and date obtained,
who it was supplied to and when, the quantity, the batch number and details of any suspected adverse reaction; show the
records to the MHRA on request; and report any serious suspected adverse reaction (regulation 170). The MHRA says this
applies to pharmacists and prescribers, not only to manufacturers.
Sources: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2012/1916/regulation/170 and GN14 section 8 (link above)

### 1.4 Route C: hold your own Manufacturer's "Specials" (MS) licence

What the MHRA says in Guidance Note 14 (GN14), section 4:

- "The manufacturer or assembler of 'specials' must hold a Manufacturer's 'Specials' Licence granted by the Licensing
  Authority."
- The site "will be inspected for compliance with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) and the conditions of the licence",
  with "a named quality controller and production manager". A Qualified Person (QP) is not required for release.
- Written records of manufacture and supply "must be kept for five years".
- An MS licence holder "may also be a registered pharmacy" using the section 10 exemption, but the labels and paperwork
  of section 10 products "should not make reference to the Manufacturer's 'Specials' Licence or number".

Source: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/645e19f5ad8a03000c38b3bc/The_supply_of_unlicensed_medicinal_products__special_GN14.pdf
(the guidance page, last updated 29 January 2025: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/supply-unlicensed-medicinal-products-specials)

**Reading:** an MS licence is what lets a compounder supply other pharmacies and clinics. It is a GMP manufacturing
operation, inspected by the MHRA, and every order it fills must still meet regulation 167.

### 1.5 The "special clinical need" test and MHRA Guidance Note 14

What GN14 says, in the MHRA's own words:

| Point | Quote | GN14 paragraph |
|---|---|---|
| The exemption is narrow | "In the interest of public health the exemption is narrowly drawn because these products, unlike licensed medicinal products, may not have been assessed by the Licensing Authority against the criteria of safety, quality and efficacy." | 1.5 |
| Licensed first | "An unlicensed medicinal product should not be supplied where an equivalent licensed medicinal product can meet the special needs of the patient." | 2.2 |
| Who decides | "Responsibility for deciding whether an individual patient has 'special needs' which a licensed product cannot meet should be a matter for the ... prescriber responsible for the patient's care." | 2.2 |
| Examples | "an intolerance or allergy to a particular ingredient, or an inability to ingest solid oral dosage forms. These examples are not exhaustive." | 2.2 |
| What does not count | "The requirement for a 'special need' relates to the special clinical needs of the individual patient. It does not include reasons of cost, convenience or operational needs". | 2.3 |
| Evidence | "MHRA expects that documentary evidence of this special need should be obtained by manufacturers, importers or distributors and that this evidence should be made available on request". It "may take the form of a prescriber's letter". | 2.3 |
| Off-label beats unlicensed | "if a UK licensed product can meet the clinical need, even off-label, it should be used instead of an unlicensed product". | 2.4 and Appendix 2 |
| Liability | "the prescriber's responsibility and potential liability are increased when prescribing off-label." | Appendix 2 |
| The specials maker checks too | Makers "should take reasonable steps to establish that persons supplied satisfy the requirements of regulation 167", for example by the person ordering "making clear that where a licensed alternative is available, why that is not clinically appropriate". | 3.2 |

Source for all rows: https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/645e19f5ad8a03000c38b3bc/The_supply_of_unlicensed_medicinal_products__special_GN14.pdf

GN14 also cites a European Court case (Commission v Poland, judgment 29 March 2012) in which the Commission argued the
exemption "does not ... justify a derogation based on financial reasons" (GN14 section 10).

**The MHRA's order of preference** (GN14 Appendix 2, "for guidance only"):

1. A UK licensed product used within its licence.
2. A UK licensed product used off-label.
3. An imported product licensed in its country of origin.
4. A UK-made special from a GMP-inspected specials manufacturer.
5. Least acceptable: a product that is not licensed, or not even classed as a medicine, in its country of origin.

### 1.6 What this means for hair loss, where licensed alternatives exist

Facts:

- Licensed UK treatments for male pattern hair loss exist: finasteride 1 mg tablets and minoxidil 5% scalp products.
  Sources: https://www.medicines.org.uk/emc/product/7068/smpc and https://www.medicines.org.uk/emc/product/5765/smpc
- No licensed topical finasteride was found on the UK medicines compendium on 23 November 2026 (see
  `clinical-men.md` in this folder). So a finasteride scalp spray, alone or mixed with minoxidil, is an unlicensed
  medicine in the UK.
- Oral minoxidil is licensed in the UK, but not for hair loss. The ASA recorded the MHRA's advice that oral minoxidil
  "was a prescription-only medicine and was licensed for the treatment of some types of hypertension only".
  Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/menwell-ltd-a25-1277445-menwell-ltd.html
- Hims & Hers UK told the ASA that "their range of treatments included both licensed prescription products and
  compounded, unlicensed prescription products". The ASA ruled on the advertising; the ruling does not say the supply
  was unlawful. Source: https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/hims---hers-uk-ltd-a22-1145016-hims--hers-uk-ltd-1.html

**Reading (the author's analysis, not a regulator's statement):**

| Reason for prescribing the compounded product | How it sits against GN14 |
|---|---|
| Documented allergy or irritation to an ingredient of the licensed minoxidil product (for example propylene glycol) | Close to the MHRA's own example ("intolerance or allergy to a particular ingredient"). Strong. |
| Licensed oral finasteride tried and stopped for side effects, or a recorded clinical reason not to take it | A clinical reason specific to the patient. Defensible if recorded. Note that topical finasteride is still absorbed and carries the same warnings (see `clinical-men.md`). |
| "One bottle is easier than two" | This is convenience. GN14 says convenience does not count. Weak. |
| "It is cheaper for us to make our own" or "it is our house product" | Cost and operational reasons. GN14 says these do not count. |
| A higher minoxidil strength (6% or 7%) because "stronger sells" | No clinical reason for the individual patient. Weak. |
| Every patient is moved to the compounded product by default | This looks like routine supply, not an individual special need. High risk. |

A service that offers the licensed options first, records for each patient why they will not do, and only then
prescribes the compounded product, fits the MHRA's and GPhC's guidance. A service whose default product is a compound
does not obviously fit it, even though such services trade in the UK today. **No MHRA or GPhC enforcement action
specifically about compounded hair-loss medicines was found in this research** (the web-search tool was unavailable for
most of it, so this is "not found", not "does not exist").
