Overview & DSM-5-TR

ADHD (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder) is a neurodevelopmental condition: it starts in childhood, while the brain is developing. It shows as a long-standing pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that gets in the way of everyday life. This module gives you the language and the rules that every later module builds on.

By the end of this module you can
  • Explain in plain words what ADHD is, and what it is not
  • Name all 18 DSM-5-TR symptoms and give a child and an adult example of each
  • Apply the diagnostic rules: symptom count, age of onset, duration, settings, impairment and exclusion
  • Apply the extra UK threshold in NICE NG87: at least moderate impairment in two or more settings
  • Record presentation, severity and remission, and use the matching ICD-11 code
  • Describe how ADHD changes across life, what happens without treatment, and who is missed

What ADHD is, in plain words

Everyone loses focus, forgets things or acts on impulse sometimes. ADHD is different in three ways:

  • It is more: it happens much more often and more strongly than in other people of the same age.
  • It is lifelong: it started in childhood and has been there ever since. It is not a recent change.
  • It costs the person: it causes real problems at home, at school or work, or in relationships.

Two short pictures make this concrete:

Child, 8. Bright, but every school report says "could do better if he concentrated". He leaves his seat, shouts out answers and loses a jumper a week. Homework takes three hours and ends in tears.
Adult, 34. Has changed jobs six times. Starts projects with energy, then leaves them unfinished. Has bills in a pile she cannot face. Her mind "never switches off", and she has always been like this.

ADHD is heterogeneous: it looks different from one person to the next, from one setting to another, and over a lifetime. There is no blood test, scan or computer test that makes the diagnosis. It is a clinical diagnosis, made from a careful history of the whole life, information from someone who knew the person as a child, and observation NICE NG87 1.3.2.

What ADHD is not. It is not a lack of intelligence, poor parenting or laziness. It is also not "everyone is a bit ADHD": the diagnosis needs many symptoms, a long history, and clear harm to the person's life.

The diagnostic rules (DSM-5-TR)

DSM-5-TR (2022) is the current edition. Its ADHD criteria are essentially the same as DSM-5 (2013); the accompanying text was updated. All five criteria, A to E, must be met.

The UK threshold is stricter. NICE NG87 1.3.3 says ADHD should be diagnosed only when the symptoms meet DSM-5 or ICD criteria and cause at least moderate psychological, social, educational or occupational impairment, and are pervasive (present in two or more important settings). Someone can meet DSM criteria with only mild impairment and still not get a diagnosis under NICE. Try this in the interactive model below. NICE NG87 1.3.3

Home

Forgetting tasks and losing belongings: keys, wallet, appointments (A1g, A1i).

Ask: "How often do you lose things you need every day?"

Study or work

Keeping attention and organising tasks: unfinished work, missed deadlines (A1b, A1d, A1e).

Ask: "What do managers or tutors say about your work?"

Relationships

Listening, waiting your turn and impulsivity: interrupting, blurting out (A1c, A2g, A2i).

Ask: "Do friends or your partner say you interrupt or do not listen?"

Watch out: problems in only one setting (for example, only at a stressful job) point to that environment, not ADHD.

How a diagnosis is made

Who can diagnose? A specialist psychiatrist, paediatrician or other appropriately qualified healthcare professional with training and expertise in diagnosing ADHD NICE NG87 1.3.1. In UK services this can include trained nurses and pharmacists working in a specialist team, where local governance allows. Diagnosis must not be made from rating scales or observation alone NICE NG87 1.3.2.

Developmental history

Old school reports, baby books and photos show whether symptoms were there before age 12 (criterion B).

Ask: "What did your school reports say about you?"

Information from others

A parent, partner or sibling can describe childhood and daily life. NICE expects this collateral history wherever possible.

Ask: "Who knew you well as a child and could talk to us?"

Everyday functioning

How symptoms affect work, home, money and relationships. This is how you judge impairment (criterion D and the NICE threshold).

Ask: "What has this cost you: jobs, relationships, money?"

Health and other explanations

Sleep, mood, anxiety, substances, thyroid and other conditions can mimic ADHD (criterion E, Module 8).

Ask: "How do you sleep? Any low mood, alcohol or drug use?"

Presentation is not a fixed type. It describes the current picture and often changes. Many children with combined presentation become predominantly inattentive adults, as visible hyperactivity turns into inner restlessness.

Predominantly inattentive

Attention, organisation and follow-through. Quiet and easily missed, especially in girls and women. Often mistaken for anxiety, depression or "not trying".

Ask: "Do you drift off in conversations or lose track of tasks?"

Predominantly hyperactive-impulsive

Restlessness, high activity and poor impulse control. More common in young children; uncommon on its own in adults.

Ask: "Do you talk over people or act before thinking?"

Combined

Both groups meet the threshold. The most common presentation in children seen in clinics. Note the stack of unfinished task cards and the jiggling foot.

Ask: "Which causes you more trouble: focus, or restlessness?"

Other specified ADHD is used when symptoms cause clear impairment but do not meet full criteria, and you state why (for example, "only 4 inattention symptoms"). Unspecified ADHD is used when you do not state the reason, often because there is not enough information yet (for example, in an emergency setting).

ICD-11 (WHO)

ICD-11 code 6A05 describes the same condition. It gives no fixed symptom count: the clinician judges whether the pattern is persistent (at least 6 months), started during development (typically early to mid-childhood), is clearly outside the normal range for age and intellectual level, and causes significant impact. NICE added ICD-11 terminology to NG87 in January 2023.

Beyond the 18 symptoms

These features are not diagnostic criteria, but they are often what patients come in with, and what bothers them most. Choose one to see what it looks like, what else it could be, and what it means for treatment.

Executive function

Executive functions are the skills that turn intentions into action: planning, starting, holding information in mind (working memory), managing time, and switching between tasks. In ADHD the knowledge is there; the self-management is not.

What patients say

"I know exactly what to do. I just cannot make myself start."

"I set six alarms and still miss things."

"Halfway through a task I forget what the next step was."

Could it be something else?
  • Low mood: no drive for anything, including things they used to enjoy; a change from their usual self
  • Learning difficulty: the problem is understanding the task, not starting or organising it
Ask this

"What happens between deciding to do a task and actually starting it?"

Why it matters for prescribing

These problems make the best treatment goals: specific, personal and measurable. Medication often helps, but skills (external reminders, breaking tasks down) are needed too.

Emotional dysregulation

Low frustration tolerance, irritability, quick anger and fast mood shifts that calm down within minutes to hours. DSM-5-TR lists these as associated features, not criteria. For many adults this is the most distressing part of ADHD.

What patients say

"I go from 0 to 100, and ten minutes later it is over."

"Little things make me snap, then I feel awful."

"I have always been like this, since I was a kid."

Could it be something else?
  • Bipolar disorder: episodes lasting days to weeks, a clear change from the usual self, reduced need for sleep
  • Personality disorder (EUPD/BPD): fear of abandonment, unstable sense of self, chronic emptiness, repeated self-harm
  • Depression: persistently low mood most of the day for 2 weeks or more
Ask this

"How long does the anger last? Has it always been like this, or did it start at a particular time?"

Why it matters for prescribing

Record mood and irritability at baseline. Stimulants often improve it, but irritability as the dose wears off (rebound) is a common side effect, so you need a baseline to compare against.

Time blindness

Difficulty sensing how long things take and how much time is left. The future feels unreal until it is urgent, so tasks are left until the deadline forces action. This is not laziness: urgency is what switches attention on.

What patients say

"I think it will take ten minutes. It takes an hour."

"I am always late, even when I really try."

"I only work when the deadline is tomorrow."

Could it be something else?
  • Anxiety-driven avoidance: the person avoids a task because it frightens them, and is often early or over-prepared elsewhere
  • Depression: slowing and lack of energy, rather than poor sense of time
Ask this

"How do you know when it is time to leave for an appointment?"

Why it matters for prescribing

Expect missed appointments and missed doses. Use reminders, simple once-daily regimens where suitable, and do not discharge someone for missing appointments without thinking about ADHD.

Hyperfocus

Intense, long focus on things that are interesting, new or rewarding, often losing track of time and needs (eating, sleeping). ADHD is a problem of regulating attention, not a lack of it: attention goes where interest is, not where it is needed.

What patients say

"I can game for eight hours but cannot read one page of a letter."

"When I am into something, the house could burn down."

"I forget to eat when I am on a project."

Could it be something else?
  • Autism (special interests): narrow, intense interests that are part of a wider pattern, with routines and distress at change or interruption
  • Hypomania: goal-directed activity with high energy, reduced need for sleep, and a change from the usual self
Ask this

"Can you stop when you need to? What happens when you are interrupted?"

Why it matters for prescribing

"They can focus on games" is not evidence against ADHD. Explain this to patients, families and colleagues, who often use it to dismiss the diagnosis.

Sleep problems

Many people with ADHD fall asleep late (a "night owl" body clock), find it hard to wind down because the mind is busy, and struggle to wake. Poor sleep also worsens attention, so the two feed each other.

What patients say

"My brain switches on at 11 pm."

"I cannot get up for work, however early I go to bed."

"I am exhausted all day but wide awake at night."

Could it be something else?
  • Obstructive sleep apnoea: loud snoring, pauses in breathing, daytime sleepiness; more likely with obesity
  • Insomnia from anxiety or depression: worry or low mood keeps them awake
  • Restless legs: an urge to move the legs at night that eases with movement
Ask this

"What time do you fall asleep on days when you do not have to get up? Do you snore?"

Why it matters for prescribing

Record sleep before any medicine. Insomnia is a common stimulant side effect; without a baseline you cannot tell whether the medicine caused it. Treat sleep disorders that mimic ADHD first.

Strengths

Many people describe energy, creativity, curiosity, quick thinking, and doing well in a crisis or a fast-moving job. These are not universal, and the evidence is mostly from people’s own accounts, but naming them builds trust and self-esteem.

What patients say

"I am the one people call in an emergency."

"I have a hundred ideas a day."

"I love jobs where every day is different."

Could it be something else?
  • Being dismissed: strengths do not rule out impairment; many people are successful and still struggling badly
  • Masking: high achievers may be hiding a huge effort to cope
Ask this

"What are you good at? When do things go well for you?"

Why it matters for prescribing

Use strengths in psychoeducation and goal-setting. A strengths-based conversation improves engagement, and diagnosis should never feel like a list of failures. Clinical practice.

Clinical pearl. Ask about all of these at the first appointment. They are often the person’s real reason for coming, they make the best treatment goals, and they give you a baseline for judging benefit and side effects later.

What untreated ADHD costs

ADHD is not "just" a concentration problem. Over a lifetime it affects almost every area of life. Each card shows the cost, and what the evidence says about treatment.

Education

Without treatmentLower grades, more exclusions and dropping out, and leaving school with fewer qualifications than ability would predict.

What treatment evidence showsIn Swedish registry data, people scored higher on university entrance tests during periods on ADHD medication than off it.

Lu et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2017

Work and money

Without treatmentMore job changes, unemployment and underemployment; problems with bills, debt and paperwork.

What treatment evidence showsFew trials measure work outcomes directly. Workplace adjustments and support are part of NICE-recommended care.

Clinical practice; NICE NG87

Injuries and driving

Without treatmentMore accidents, emergency visits and road traffic crashes. Accidents drive the doubled mortality.

What treatment evidence showsSerious road crashes were less likely during months on ADHD medication than off it, in the same people.

Chang et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2017; Dalsgaard 2015

Substance use

Without treatmentHigher rates of smoking, alcohol and drug misuse, often starting younger.

What treatment evidence showsTreating ADHD with stimulants is not linked to more later substance misuse; substance-related events were less likely during medicated periods.

Quinn et al., Am J Psychiatry 2017

Justice system

Without treatmentMore arrests and convictions, often linked to impulsivity.

What treatment evidence showsConvictions were about a third lower in men during periods on medication than off it, and lower in women too.

Lichtenstein et al., NEJM 2012

Relationships and mental health

Without treatmentMore relationship breakdown, low self-esteem, depression and anxiety, often after years of feeling a failure.

What treatment evidence showsDiagnosis and psychoeducation alone often bring relief ("it was not my fault"). Coexisting conditions need their own treatment.

Clinical practice; NICE NG87

How to read this evidence. These long-term studies are observational: they compare the same people during periods on and off medication. That removes many differences between people, but it still shows association, not proof of cause. Short-term randomised trials give the strongest evidence for symptom benefit. A good specialist explains both honestly and never over-promises.

The same person across life

Childhood

Visible fidgeting and attention difficulties. Adults (parents, teachers) provide structure, which can hide how severe the problem is.

Ask: "What did teachers say? Did you need more help than your siblings?"

Adolescence

Planning and organisational demands rise sharply (exams, independent study). Many people first struggle here, especially bright students who coped at primary school.

Ask: "When did school start to feel much harder?"

Adulthood

Restlessness becomes inner (a mind that will not switch off), and the problems shift to work, money, driving and relationships. Fewer symptoms are needed for diagnosis from age 17.

Ask: "Have you always been like this, or did it start recently?"

A changing presentation does not tell the whole story. A "late" first presentation in adulthood is usually lifelong ADHD that was compensated for until demands rose. Truly new symptoms in adulthood need other causes ruled out first (Module 8).

In the UK, diagnosis and prescribing have risen sharply over the last decade, and waiting lists are long. A good specialist neither dismisses ADHD nor over-diagnoses it: they assess carefully and explain the evidence honestly.

Who gets missed

NICE NG87 1.2.1 asks professionals to be aware that ADHD may be more common in:

  • Born preterm
  • Looked-after childrenand young people
  • ODD or conduct disorderchildren and young people
  • Mood disorderssuch as anxiety or depression, in children and young people
  • Family historya close relative with ADHD
  • Epilepsy
  • Other neurodevelopmental conditionsautism, tics, intellectual disability, specific learning difficulties
  • Adult mental health conditions
  • Substance misusecurrent or past
  • Criminal justice contactyouth or adult system
  • Acquired brain injurycheck timing of onset

Girls and women are under-recognised: they are less likely to be referred, more likely to have undiagnosed ADHD, and more likely to be given another diagnosis instead, such as anxiety, depression or personality disorder NICE NG87 1.2.2. Inattention and masking (working hard to hide difficulties) make ADHD less visible. Section D (Special presentations) covers this in depth.

NICE also says do not run universal screening for ADHD in nurseries or schools NICE NG87 1.2.3.

Deep dive: debates a specialist should be able to discuss
  • Relative age effect: the youngest children in a school year are more likely to be diagnosed and treated. Some normal immaturity may be labelled ADHD, so always compare with same-age peers.
  • Private diagnoses and shared care: assessment quality varies, so some NHS areas review private diagnoses before agreeing shared care (Module 6).
  • Long-term benefit: short-term trial evidence for medication is strong. Long-term outcome data are mostly observational.
  • Late-onset ADHD: adults with no childhood history need careful exclusion of other causes, such as substance use, mood or anxiety disorders, sleep disorders and brain injury. Do not diagnose ADHD until these are ruled out.

Recap and practise

5 things to remember

  1. ADHD is more, lifelong and costly. Many symptoms, since childhood, causing real harm. No test diagnoses it: the history does.
  2. Know the 18 symptoms. 9 inattention (A1) and 9 hyperactivity-impulsivity (A2). You need 6 in a domain, or 5 from age 17.
  3. All five criteria must be met. Symptoms (A), onset before 12 (B), 2+ settings (C), impairment (D), not better explained (E).
  4. NICE is stricter. At least moderate impairment, pervasive across 2+ important settings (NG87 1.3.3).
  5. Think of who gets missed. Girls and women, and the NG87 1.2.1 groups. Untreated ADHD has real costs, including twice the mortality.

ADHD is a brain-based condition. Genes explain most of the risk, and differences in how brain networks use two chemical messengers, dopamine and noradrenaline, explain many symptoms. Every licensed ADHD medicine works on these messengers. This module explains how, in plain words, so you can predict how each medicine behaves and explain it to patients.

What causes ADHD

In plain words: ADHD is mostly inherited. Many genes each add a tiny amount of risk, and some events before or after birth add more. It is not caused by parenting, sugar or laziness.

~74%of the difference between people in ADHD traits is explained by genes (heritability)Faraone & Larsson 2019 (twin studies)
27gene regions linked to ADHD so far; each has a tiny effect. There is no single "ADHD gene" and no genetic test.Demontis et al. 2023
~3 yrslater peak in cortical thickness in children with ADHD: brain development is delayed, not abnormalShaw et al. 2007

Things that add risk

Genes are the main cause. These factors add risk on top, and the evidence for each is not equally strong.

Clear link
  • Preterm birth and low birth weightThe earlier the birth, the higher the risk. NICE lists preterm birth as a high-prevalence group.
  • Alcohol in pregnancyFetal alcohol spectrum disorder often includes attention and impulse problems.
  • Severe early deprivationFor example, long institutional care in early childhood.
Likely, check the details
  • Acquired brain injuryAsk when symptoms started: before or after the injury.
  • Toxins such as leadChildhood exposure is linked to attention problems.
Linked, but mostly shared genes
  • Smoking in pregnancySibling studies suggest the link is largely explained by genes the family shares, not the smoking itself.

Myths and facts

MythBad parenting causes ADHD.Parenting does not cause ADHD. A calm, structured home helps every child with it, and parent training is offered as support, not blame.
MythSugar makes children hyperactive.Controlled trials found no effect of sugar on behaviour or attention (Wolraich 1995).
Not shownScreens cause ADHD.Heavy screen use and ADHD go together, but that may be because ADHD draws people to screens. Cause has not been shown.
FactADHD runs in families.About 74% heritable. A parent or sibling with ADHD is one of the strongest clues.
MythThey just need to try harder.The problem is regulating effort and attention, not wanting to succeed. Many people try much harder than others.
PartlyAdults grow out of it.Most improve, but about 65% still have impairing symptoms at 25 (Faraone 2006).
How to explain it to a patient

"ADHD runs in families. Your brain develops and uses its chemical messengers a little differently. It is nobody’s fault, and it is very treatable."

The ADHD brain

Brain scans of large groups show that four networks work a little differently in ADHD. Think of them as teams that must hand over to each other smoothly.

Executive control networkPrefrontal cortex, at the front

What it doesPlans, holds information in mind and puts the brakes on impulses.

In ADHDWeaker control: forgetting what you were doing, acting before thinking.

Reward networkStriatum, deep in the brain

What it doesDecides what is worth doing now, using dopamine.

A scan cannot diagnose ADHD. These are average differences between large groups. They overlap heavily with people without ADHD, so no scan, blood test or brain biomarker can diagnose an individual. Diagnosis stays clinical (NICE NG87).

Dopamine and noradrenaline: what they do

Brain cells pass signals with chemical messengers. Two of them, dopamine and noradrenaline (together called catecholamines), tune the prefrontal cortex, the part of the brain that plans, focuses and puts the brakes on. Every licensed ADHD medicine works by boosting them.

Signal and noise: the key idea

Think of the prefrontal cortex as a radio trying to stay on the right station.

Noradrenaline turns up the signal

Through α2A receptors it strengthens the connections between prefrontal cells that hold the task in mind, so the right "station" comes through clearly.

Dopamine turns down the noise

Through D1 receptors it quietens irrelevant inputs, so distractions do not break in.

When either is too weak, the signal fades and the noise wins: the person drifts off, forgets the plan or reacts to every distraction. Both systems work best at a moderate level, which is the inverted U you will try in lesson 6.

What is different in ADHD?

  • Weaker signalling, not "empty" brainsThe circuits that use these messengers work less efficiently on average. It is not simply "low dopamine".
  • Reward feels differentBrain-imaging (PET) studies in adults found fewer dopamine receptors and transporters in the reward pathway (Volkow 2009), which fits with low motivation for slow, boring rewards.
  • Urgency and novelty helpDeadlines, novelty and interest all release catecholamines. That is why people with ADHD often only "switch on" at the last minute.
  • Stress makes it worseStress floods the prefrontal cortex with catecholamines and pushes it past the peak, so performance falls apart under pressure.
Avoid the "low dopamine" myth. Telling patients "you just lack dopamine" is too simple and can suggest they should seek stimulation. Say instead: "the messengers that help your brain focus and filter work less efficiently, and the medicine helps them work better."

The key players: transporters and receptors

To understand any ADHD medicine you need to know six "players" at the synapse. Each medicine acts on one or more of them, and that explains how it helps and why it causes the side effects it does. Choose a player.

The synapse

The tiny gap between two nerve cells. One cell releases a chemical messenger into the gap; the next cell picks it up.

Where it is

Everywhere in the brain, and on the nerves that control the heart, blood vessels and gut.

Its job

Passes signals on. How strong and how long the signal lasts decides whether the message gets through clearly.

What ADHD medicines do to it

Every ADHD medicine acts here. None of them adds anything new to the brain: they change how strongly and how long the brain’s own messengers work.

What that means for the patient

This is a useful reassurance: the medicine does not change who you are, it helps your own signals work better.

Everyday pictureA conversation across a narrow corridor: what matters is how loudly and how long each word is heard.

VMAT2

Vesicular MonoAmine Transporter 2: a pump on the wall of the tiny storage bubbles (vesicles) inside the nerve ending.

Where it is

Inside dopamine and noradrenaline nerve endings (and serotonin ones).

Its job

Packs dopamine and noradrenaline into vesicles, ready to be released when the nerve fires.

What ADHD medicines do to it

Amfetamines disrupt VMAT2, so messenger leaks out of the vesicles into the cell and is then pushed out into the gap. Methylphenidate, atomoxetine and guanfacine do not act here.

What that means for the patient

This is why amfetamines give a bigger rise in dopamine than methylphenidate, and why immediate-release dexamfetamine has the highest misuse potential.

Everyday picturePacking letters into envelopes, ready to post.

DAT

The dopamine transporter (DA = dopamine, T = transporter): a protein pump in the wall of the nerve ending.

Where it is

Dense in the striatum and the nucleus accumbens (the reward centre). There are very few in the prefrontal cortex.

Its job

Sucks dopamine back out of the gap into the nerve ending within milliseconds, which ends the signal. The dopamine is then reused.

What ADHD medicines do to it

Methylphenidate blocks it (a plug in the pump). Amfetamines ride in through it and run it backwards, pushing dopamine out. Atomoxetine and guanfacine do not act on it.

What that means for the patient

Blocking DAT in the reward centre gives a fast dopamine rise, which explains the misuse potential and controlled drug status of stimulants. Dopamine in the striatum also explains why tics can appear or worsen.

Everyday pictureA recycling vacuum that clears the messenger away. Block the vacuum and the message stays longer.

NET

The norepinephrine transporter (norepinephrine is the American name for noradrenaline): the same kind of pump, for noradrenaline.

Where it is

On noradrenaline nerve endings across the whole cortex, including the prefrontal cortex, and on the nerves that control the heart and blood vessels.

Its job

Clears noradrenaline from the gap. In the prefrontal cortex, where there are few DAT pumps, NET also clears most of the dopamine.

What ADHD medicines do to it

Methylphenidate blocks it, amfetamines reverse it, and atomoxetine blocks only NET.

What that means for the patient

More noradrenaline at the heart and vessels raises pulse and blood pressure, so these are monitored with stimulants and atomoxetine. Because atomoxetine blocks only NET, it raises dopamine in the prefrontal cortex but not in the reward centre, so it is not a controlled drug.

Everyday pictureA second vacuum, for a different messenger, that also tidies up dopamine in the planning part of the brain.

D1 receptor

A receptor on the receiving cell. When dopamine docks, it changes how that cell behaves.

Where it is

On prefrontal cortex cells (D1). In the striatum, D2 receptors matter more.

Its job

Turns down the noise: helps the prefrontal cortex ignore irrelevant inputs and distractions. It works best at a moderate level; too much switches the prefrontal cortex off (the inverted U).

What ADHD medicines do to it

No ADHD medicine docks on D1 directly. Stimulants and atomoxetine raise the dopamine that reaches it.

What that means for the patient

Explains why the right dose matters: too little and distractions win; too much and the person becomes flat, rigid or irritable.

Everyday pictureA letterbox: the message only counts when it is delivered through the right door.

α2A receptor

A receptor for noradrenaline that responds to quite low levels (it is "high affinity").

Where it is

On the tiny spines of prefrontal cortex cells, and in the brainstem, where it calms the body’s fight-or-flight system.

Its job

In the prefrontal cortex it turns up the signal, helping cells hold information in mind. In the brainstem it lowers pulse and blood pressure.

What ADHD medicines do to it

Guanfacine docks on it directly. Stimulants and atomoxetine raise the noradrenaline that reaches it.

What that means for the patient

Explains guanfacine’s side effects: sleepiness, low blood pressure and a slow pulse. It also explains why guanfacine must be tapered: stopping suddenly can cause rebound high blood pressure.

Everyday pictureA volume knob for the right signal.

The whole story in four lines

Vesicles (filled by VMAT2) release dopamine and noradrenaline into the synapse. They dock on receptors (D1, α2A) to pass the message on. Recycling pumps (DAT, NET) clear them away to end the signal. ADHD medicines keep more messenger at the receptors for longer, or act on the receptor directly.

Four routes to the same goal

Every licensed ADHD medicine boosts dopamine and/or noradrenaline signalling in the prefrontal cortex, but by a different route. That route explains how fast it works, how long it lasts and its side effects.

Methylphenidate

Dexamfetamine and lisdexamfetamine

Atomoxetine

Same messengers, outside the attention circuit

The medicines cannot target only the prefrontal cortex. The same messengers work all over the brain and body, so the side effects are predictable from the mechanism. This is why NICE asks you to monitor pulse, blood pressure, weight, sleep and mood.

Heart and blood vessels

WhyNoradrenaline drives heart rate and blood pressure.

What you see and doRaised pulse and blood pressure. Measure before starting and after every dose change (NICE NG87).

Appetite

WhyCatecholamines in the hypothalamus reduce hunger.

What you see and doReduced appetite and weight loss. Monitor weight (and height in children); eat a good breakfast before the dose.

Sleep and arousal

WhyBoth messengers keep the brain awake and alert.

What you see and doDifficulty falling asleep, especially if the dose lasts into the evening. Record sleep at baseline.

Movement

WhyDopamine in the striatum controls movement and habits.

What you see and doTics can appear or worsen. Ask at every review.

Reward

WhyFast dopamine rises in the nucleus accumbens feel rewarding.

What you see and doMisuse and diversion potential, highest with immediate-release stimulants. That is why stimulants are controlled drugs.

Mood

WhyToo much catecholamine pushes the brain past the peak.

What you see and doIrritability, anxiety, a "flat" feeling; rarely psychosis or mania. Think "dose too high".

Try it: finding the right dose

More is not always better. The prefrontal cortex works best with a moderate level of dopamine and noradrenaline. This "inverted U" explains why we titrate slowly, why too high a dose makes people worse, and why stress can make symptoms flare.

Clinical pearl. If someone becomes irritable, flat or anxious after a dose increase, think "past the peak" before "needs more". Reduce, do not escalate, and discuss with the prescriber.

Raised pulse and blood pressure, reduced appetite, trouble getting to sleep if cover runs late, and irritability as it wears off ("rebound").

Practical point

Few drug interactions because it is not cleared by CYP enzymes; never combine with an MAOI. If cover ends too early, the answer is usually a different formulation or timing, not a bigger dose.

In clinic

What the patient notices

A stronger effect per milligram than methylphenidate. Lisdexamfetamine rises and falls smoothly, so many people notice less of a sharp "wear-off".

Practical point

  2. cAMP opens HCN channels and the signal leaks. When cAMP is high (for example under stress or with low noradrenergic tone), HCN channels near the synapse open. Current leaks out and the network signal fades.
  3. Guanfacine stimulates α2A receptors. Guanfacine is a selective α2A agonist, with 15–20 times higher affinity for α2A than for α2B/α2C (Intuniv SPC §5.1). α2A couples to Gi, which inhibits adenylyl cyclase (AC), so cAMP falls.

Deep dive: pharmacokinetics that matter in practice
  • Methylphenidate: short half-life (~2–3 h); formulations (IR, OROS, beaded MR) determine profile. Metabolised mainly by carboxylesterase CES1 — not CYP-dependent.
  • Lisdexamfetamine: conversion is rate-limited, giving low between-patient variability; not affected by GI pH in the way amfetamine salts are.
  • Amfetamines: renal excretion increases in acidic urine (e.g. high-dose vitamin C) and decreases in alkaline urine (e.g. sodium bicarbonate) {SPC}.
  • Atomoxetine: CYP2D6 — poor metabolisers (and those taking strong CYP2D6 inhibitors such as fluoxetine or paroxetine) have markedly higher exposure (SPC: ~6–8-fold AUC with strong inhibitors at steady state).
  • Guanfacine: CYP3A4 — halve the dose with moderate/strong CYP3A4 inhibitors; increase with inducers {SPC}.
How to explain it to a patient

"Your brain uses two messengers, dopamine and noradrenaline, to focus and to put the brakes on. In ADHD the signal is a bit weak. This medicine helps the signal stay strong for a few hours, like turning up the volume on the part of your brain that plans and concentrates. It does not change who you are, and we will find the dose that works for you step by step."

Recap and practise

5 things to remember

  1. ADHD is mostly genetic. About 74% heritable, many genes of tiny effect. Not caused by parenting or sugar.
  2. Networks, not one spot. Executive control, reward, default mode and timing networks differ on average. No scan can diagnose it.
  3. Two messengers. Dopamine ("worth doing") and noradrenaline ("pay attention") tune the prefrontal cortex.
  4. The inverted U. Too little and too much both impair performance, so titrate carefully and reduce if the person gets worse.
  5. Mechanism predicts practice. Reuptake block or release (stimulants, fast, CD) vs NET block or α2A agonism (non-stimulants, slow, not CD).

Recognition and referral: adults

Adults often come forward after a child is diagnosed, after a crisis at work or in a relationship, or after reading about ADHD. NICE sets out who to refer.

Teaching

Two systems, one pattern

DSM-5-TR (American Psychiatric Association, 2022) gives a symptom count and five criteria, A to E. ICD-11 (WHO) describes the same pattern without a fixed count and codes the presentation as 6A05.0, .1, .2, .Y or .Z. NICE NG87 1.3.3 accepts either, and adds that symptoms must cause at least moderate impairment across multiple settings.

  1. Gate A, symptoms: 6 or more of 9 in either domain (5 at 17 and over), for 6 months, beyond developmental level.
  2. Gate B, onset: several symptoms before 12. Collateral history, school reports and old records matter most here.
  3. Gate C, settings: several symptoms in two or more settings. A child who is only “bad” at home has not passed this gate.
  4. Gate D, impairment: clear interference with social, school or work life. A high score with no real cost does not count.
  5. Gate E, not better explained: anxiety, depression, trauma, sleep disorders, substances and other conditions considered first (see Module 8).

Teaching

Does it count? Six questions

Every behaviour on a rating scale is common. It only counts towards ADHD when it passes all six questions in the figure. Work from top to bottom:

  1. Often and persistent: most days, for at least 6 months. A bad fortnight after a bereavement does not count.
  2. Beyond developmental level: a 4-year-old who cannot sit through a 30-minute assembly is normal; a 10-year-old who cannot sit for 5 minutes is not.
  3. Two or more settings: seen at home and school, or at work and with friends.
  4. Impairment: lost marks, warnings, accidents, damaged relationships; not just “annoying”.
  5. Not better explained: worry, low mood, sleep loss, trauma, substances, hearing or learning problems.

Adults without a childhood diagnosis should be referred to a specialist trained in ADHD when typical symptoms began in childhood, have persisted, and are not explained by another condition (NG87 1.2.10). The fading bar in the figure is a general pattern, not data.

3. Autism and intellectual disability

Judging symptoms against developmental level

  • Autism: NG87 1.3.3 says exclusion based on a pervasive developmental disorder is not recommended. Both can be diagnosed. Ask whether inattention is broad (ADHD) or limited to non-preferred tasks because of focused interests (autism), and whether restlessness is constant or linked to sensory overload or changes in routine.
  • Intellectual disability: ICD-11 6A05 requires symptoms outside the limits of normal variation for age and level of intellectual functioning. Compare with people of the same developmental level, not the same age. DIVA-5 ID is an adapted interview for adults with intellectual disability (DIVA Foundation).
  • Treatment: offer the same medication choices (NG87 1.7.18), but titrate more slowly and monitor more often with neurodevelopmental disorders such as autism, tics or learning disability (1.7.28).
  • Communication: use visual supports, shorter sessions, carers who know the person well, and observation in familiar settings.

Example: Sian, 24, has a mild intellectual disability. At the day centre she cannot stay with a 5-minute task that peers of the same ability manage, loses belongings daily and interrupts constantly; her mother describes the same since primary school. This counts. If she only struggled with tasks beyond her ability, it would not.

4. Older adults

ADHD after 60: diagnosis and medicines

NG87 1.3.5 applies to all age groups. In older adults, separate lifelong ADHD from new cognitive decline: ask about childhood (collateral from siblings, school reports), and screen for memory problems, depression, sleep problems, hearing and vision, and medicines that affect attention. New-onset problems in later life suggest another cause.

The NICE treatment pathway

NG87 puts information, support and environmental modifications first, then a stepped medication sequence with defined decision points.

Learning objectives
  • Describe non-pharmacological care for under-5s, children and adults
  • Apply the NG87 medication sequence for children and adults
  • Explain the "6-week adequate trial" rule
  • Know where tertiary advice is needed
Teaching

Everyone: information, support and environmental modifications

Give tailored information about ADHD and its treatments; involve the person (and family) in a shared decision. Environmental modifications reduce the impact of symptoms — e.g. seating, lighting, noise, shorter tasks, movement breaks, structure at school or work. NICE advises these be implemented and reviewed before starting medication in children and adults.

Under 5 years NICE NG87

Offer an ADHD-focused group parent-training programme first-line (1.5.7). Medication in under-5s only after a second specialist opinion from an ADHD service with expertise in young children (1.5.9). Most ADHD medicines are not licensed below 6 years.

Children aged 5+ and young people

Group parent-training/education is offered to parents of children with ADHD, particularly with co-existing oppositional or conduct problems. CBT may be considered for young people who have benefited from medication but still have impairing symptoms. Medication is offered if ADHD symptoms still cause persistent significant impairment in at least one domain after environmental modifications.

Adults

Medication is first-line where symptoms cause significant impairment after environmental modifications. Consider non-pharmacological treatment (e.g. structured supportive psychological intervention focused on ADHD, CBT) for adults who choose not to take medication, have difficulty adhering, or for whom medication was ineffective or not tolerated (NG87 1.5.16; minimum content of a structured supportive psychological intervention plus regular follow-up, 1.5.18).

Benefits and harms — the conversation before any treatment NICE NG87

NG87 1.5.4. Before starting any treatment, discuss with the person (and family or carers), encouraging children and young people to give their own account: the benefits and harms of non-pharmacological and pharmacological treatments — for example the efficacy of medication compared with no treatment or non-drug treatments, potential adverse effects and non-response rates; the benefits of a healthy lifestyle including exercise; and their preferences and concerns.
  • Benefit: stimulants usually work within days of reaching an effective dose, but not everyone responds — which is why NICE builds in switches after 6-week trials and a second opinion if there is no response to stimulants and a non-stimulant (1.7.16).
  • Growth: stimulants can slow height and weight gain in children; the average effect on height is generally reported as modest, but it varies, so height is plotted 6-monthly and planned breaks are considered if height is significantly affected (1.8.5, 1.8.7). Size of effect not verified live
  • Cardiovascular: stimulants and atomoxetine raise average heart rate and blood pressure a little; long-term observational studies suggest a small increase in cardiovascular risk (mainly hypertension) with longer use, so HR and BP are checked before and after each dose change and every 6 months (1.8.9–1.8.11). Magnitude not verified live
  • Non-drug options remain first-line for under-5s (1.5.7) and are valid choices for adults who prefer them (1.5.16).

The 6-week rule

Adequate trial. NICE uses a 6-week trial at an adequate dose before concluding a medicine has not worked (1.7.8, 1.7.12). "Adequate" means titrated to the optimal tolerated dose — a few weeks at a starting dose is not a failed trial. SPCs separately advise stopping if there is no improvement after appropriate dose adjustment over about 1 month.

Diet

Do not advise eliminating artificial colourings and additives as a general treatment (1.6.2), and do not advise or offer omega-3/6 fatty acid supplements for treating ADHD (1.6.4). Ask whether particular foods or drinks seem to affect behaviour: if there is a clear link, advise a food-and-behaviour diary, and if the diary supports a relationship, offer referral to a dietitian, with any specific elimination diet managed jointly by the dietitian, specialist and family (1.6.3). Tell families there is no evidence on the long-term effectiveness or harms of a "few food" diet and only limited evidence of short-term benefit (1.6.5). Encourage a balanced diet, good nutrition and regular exercise.

Deep dive: Why lisdexamfetamine first in adults is debated

NICE allows either lisdexamfetamine or methylphenidate first in adults, reflecting network meta-analysis data (amfetamines slightly more efficacious in adults; methylphenidate best balance in children) and cost. Locally, formulary choice, shortages and patient preference drive selection.

Beyond medication

Non-pharmacological support in depth

Parents and carers

Under 5: an ADHD-focused group parent-training programme is first-line (NG87 1.5.7). With ODD or conduct symptoms in children 5 and over, offer parents a parent-training programme (1.5.11); consider individual programmes when group sessions are hard to attend, for example because of disability, language, learning disability, parental ill-health or transport (1.5.12).

Young people

If medication has helped but impairment remains, consider a course of CBT covering social skills with peers, problem-solving, self-control, active listening and dealing with and expressing feelings (NG87 1.5.14).

Adults

Consider non-pharmacological treatment for adults who choose not to take medication, struggle to adhere, or find it ineffective or intolerable (1.5.16), or alongside medication when impairment remains (1.5.17). As a minimum: a structured supportive psychological intervention focused on ADHD with regular follow-up; it may include CBT (1.5.18).

Diet, sleep and exercise

Stress a balanced diet, good nutrition and regular exercise (1.6.1). Do not advise eliminating artificial colourings and additives as a general treatment (1.6.2) or fatty acid supplements (1.6.4). Ask about foods that seem to affect behaviour; if there is a clear link, use a food and behaviour diary and refer to a dietitian if it supports the link (1.6.3).

School and college

With consent, contact the school, college or university at diagnosis, when symptoms change and at transitions, to explain the diagnosis, its likely effects, coexisting conditions and the treatment plan (1.4.12). Use the letter builder below.

Work

Employers must make reasonable adjustments. Access to Work can fund practical support (for example equipment, assistive software or a job coach) and mental health support at work; it does not pay for reasonable adjustments (GOV.UK, Access to Work, checked 21 Oct 2026).

Principles of titration (NG87)

  • Titrate against symptoms and adverse effects in line with the BNF or BNFC until dose optimisation is achieved (1.7.27); prescribers should know the controlled drug legislation (1.7.25); weekly contact with the specialist team during titration is suggested (1.7.26).

  • Prescribe MR methylphenidate by brand (MHRA Drug Safety Update, September 2022): release profiles differ.

Annual review NICE NG87

A healthcare professional with training and expertise in ADHD should review ADHD medication at least once a year and discuss with the person (and family) whether it should continue. The review should include preference, benefits, adverse effects, clinical need, effect of missed doses, planned dose reductions or periods of no treatment, physical and mental health, and need for psychological, educational or occupational support. NICE suggests considering a trial without medication or a dose reduction where appropriate to assess ongoing need — e.g. a "treatment break" timed to avoid exams (1.10.1–1.10.3). If medication is continued, document the reasons (1.10.3).

Shared care NICE NG87

After titration and dose stabilisation, prescribing and monitoring should be carried out under a shared care protocol with primary care (1.7.29). The specialist initiates, titrates and retains responsibility for review; primary care continues prescribing and routine monitoring and refers back with concerns.

Driving

In the UK, it is an offence to drive with certain specified drugs above set limits; amfetamine (including dexamfetamine and lisdexamfetamine, which is converted to dexamfetamine) is one of them. There is a medical defence if the medicine was prescribed, taken as directed, and driving is not impaired. Methylphenidate is not a "specified drug" but the general offence of driving while impaired still applies. People with ADHD must tell the DVLA if it affects their ability to drive safely. Penalties for drug driving include a minimum 1-year ban, an unlimited fine, up to 6 months in prison and a criminal record.

Travel

GOV.UK ("Take medicine in or out of the UK", checked 20 Oct 2026): medicine containing a controlled drug must be carried in hand luggage when entering or leaving the UK, and may be taken away at the border if the person cannot prove it was prescribed for them. Taking medicine out of the UK: check whether it contains a CD, check the destination country's rules with its embassy before travelling, and carry proof that it is yours — a prescription or a letter from the doctor. Keep medicines in their original labelled packaging. Some countries restrict or ban amfetamines. Bringing medicine into the UK: non-UK residents need a letter of proof and may bring up to 3 months' supply (more only with a personal licence, in exceptional circumstances). UK residents who were prescribed medicine abroad should contact the Home Office Drug and Firearms Licensing Unit (DFLU) (GOV.UK).

Safety cards

Heart, seizures, eating disorders, pregnancy and driving

Heart

Before starting: refer for a cardiology opinion if any NG87 1.7.5 feature is present (for example congenital heart disease, sudden cardiac death in a first-degree relative under 40, exertional breathlessness or fainting, sudden-onset rapid regular palpitations, cardiac-type chest pain, heart failure signs, a murmur, or hypertensive BP in adults). Children with BP consistently above the 95th centile: paediatric hypertension specialist first (1.7.6).

On treatment: HR and BP before and after each dose change and every 6 months (1.8.9). Sustained resting HR over 120, arrhythmia, or SBP above the 95th centile (or a clinically significant increase) on 2 occasions: reduce the dose and refer (1.8.11). Methylphenidate SmPC 4.3 lists pre-existing cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disorders as contraindications.

Seizures

People with epilepsy may have increased prevalence of ADHD (NG87 1.2.1). Methylphenidate SmPC 4.4 advises caution in epilepsy and says it should be discontinued if seizure frequency increases or new-onset seizures occur (Concerta XL SmPC). If new seizures appear or existing seizures worsen, review ADHD medication and stop any that might contribute; after investigation, cautiously reintroduce if it is unlikely to be the cause (1.8.16).

Eating disorders and weight

Methylphenidate SmPC 4.3 lists anorexia nervosa or anorexic disorders as a contraindication. Stimulants reduce appetite: monitor weight as in NG87 1.8.5 and use 1.8.6 strategies. Ask about restriction, bingeing and the wish to lose weight, which also raises diversion risk (1.7.23).

Pregnancy

The UKTIS Bumps leaflet on methylphenidate (January 2023, version 4) says it is occasionally used in pregnancy when needed. Some studies suggest a higher chance of miscarriage and some heart defects after early-pregnancy use, though most exposed babies have no birth defect and causation is uncertain. It has been linked to reduced fetal growth, and babies may need monitoring for withdrawal after late-pregnancy use. Women should not stop or reduce it without advice; review early if planning or in an unplanned pregnancy. Clinicians can use uktis.org for detail.

Driving and the DVLA

GOV.UK (checked 21 Oct 2026): people must tell DVLA if their ADHD or ADHD medication affects their ability to drive safely; if driving is not affected they do not need to. Not telling DVLA about ADHD that affects safe driving can lead to a fine of up to £1,000. Car or motorcycle licence holders report online or on form A1. Bus, coach or lorry (Group 2) licence holders use form A1V. Learner drivers do not usually need to tell DVLA unless a doctor has said their ADHD or medication affects safe driving. NG87 1.4.3 includes the same duty in the post-diagnosis discussion. The separate drug-driving law is covered above.

Counselling essentials

  • What and why: which brand, expected benefits, time to effect (stimulants same day; atomoxetine/guanfacine weeks).
  • How: time of day (morning; avoid late doses), food rules per brand, swallow whole (Concerta-type) vs sprinkle (Medikinet XL, Equasym XL, Meflynate XL, Elvanse dispersed).
  • Side effects: appetite, sleep, headache, palpitations; what to report (chest pain, fainting, mood change, tics, suicidal thoughts on atomoxetine, jaundice).

  • Driving and travel: medical defence; letters for travel.
  • Missed doses: skip if late in the day; do not double.
  • Stopping: methylphenidate SPCs do not require a routine taper, but advise careful supervision during withdrawal because it may unmask depression or chronic over-activity (Concerta XL SPC §4.4) — monitor mood. Taper dexamfetamine (SPC) and guanfacine (≤1 mg every 3–7 days; rebound hypertension). Atomoxetine: no mandatory taper.
  • Planned surgery: tell the surgeon and anaesthetist; methylphenidate should not be taken on the day of surgery when halogenated anaesthetics may be used (risk of sudden BP and heart-rate rise; SPC §4.5).
Screen for contraindications at every new supply or medicine review (SPC §4.3; see Module 5): glaucoma, phaeochromocytoma, MAOI use within 14 days, hyperthyroidism, uncontrolled hypertension or symptomatic cardiovascular/cerebrovascular disease, active psychosis, mania, severe depression or suicidal thoughts, and anorexia. 1. Every product at a glance

The UK has seven 22:78-type methylphenidate tablets, four bioequivalent 50:50 capsules, Medikinet XL, Equasym XL, two immediate-release brands, lisdexamfetamine, dexamfetamine, atomoxetine and guanfacine. They differ in how they release the drug, and therefore in when they work, how they can be taken and what they can be swapped with.

  • Not every 22:78-type tablet is osmotic. Concerta XL, Affenid XL, Atenza XL and Matoride XL use an osmotic shell that may appear in stools. Xaggitin XL is a matrix tablet (25:75) and Xenidate XL is a multiple-unit tablet; they leave no shell (SmPC 4.4, 5.2, 6.1; SPS).
  • Capsules can usually be opened and sprinkled on cold soft food (Ritalin XL-type, Medikinet XL, Equasym XL); tablets must be swallowed whole (SmPC 4.2).
  • Food matters for two products: Medikinet XL with or after breakfast; Equasym XL before breakfast. Intuniv should not be taken with a high-fat meal (SmPC 4.2).
  • Licences differ at 18: see section 5.

2. Switching between brands, formulations and medicines

Work through these steps every time a switch is proposed, for example during a shortage.

  1. Is the target in the same SPS bioequivalence group? If yes, switch at the same dose and quantity. If the prescription names a brand, a different brand still needs a new prescription.
  2. Is Medikinet XL involved? Switching to or from the capsule group is possible but needs particular care: Medikinet XL is not completely bioequivalent with the other four (SPS).
  3. Is Equasym XL involved? No other MR product is bioequivalent; do not switch it or prescribe it generically (SPS).
  4. Tablet ↔ capsule, or MR ↔ IR? No SmPC or SPS dose conversion exists between these groups. SPS relates some products to IR methylphenidate (table below) for reference only; the specialist chooses the starting dose of the new product and titrates.
  5. Different molecule? There is no conversion factor. Start the new medicine at its own licensed starting dose and titrate, following the NG87 sequence (1.7.7–1.7.15).
  6. Counsel and follow up on what looks or works differently, and review effect, HR, BP and sleep after the switch.

Rebound is the return of symptoms, sometimes with irritability, as a dose wears off. First check the dose time, whether the product's cover (about 4 h IR, 8 h for 50:50 and 30:70 capsules, 12 h for 22:78 tablets; SPS) matches the person's day, and food (Medikinet XL).
