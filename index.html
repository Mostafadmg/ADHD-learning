Overview & DSM-5-TR

  • ~50 min in 10 short lessons

ADHD (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder) is a neurodevelopmental condition: it starts in childhood, while the brain is developing. It shows as a long-standing pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that gets in the way of everyday life. This module gives you the language and the rules that every later module builds on.

By the end of this module you can
  • Explain in plain words what ADHD is, and what it is not
  • Name all 18 DSM-5-TR symptoms and give a child and an adult example of each
  • Apply the diagnostic rules: symptom count, age of onset, duration, settings, impairment and exclusion
  • Apply the extra UK threshold in NICE NG87: at least moderate impairment in two or more settings
  • Record presentation, severity and remission, and use the matching ICD-11 code
  • Describe how ADHD changes across life, what happens without treatment, and who is missed

What ADHD is, in plain words

Everyone loses focus, forgets things or acts on impulse sometimes. ADHD is different in three ways:

  • It is more: it happens much more often and more strongly than in other people of the same age.
  • It is lifelong: it started in childhood and has been there ever since. It is not a recent change.
  • It costs the person: it causes real problems at home, at school or work, or in relationships.

Two short pictures make this concrete:

Child, 8. Bright, but every school report says "could do better if he concentrated". He leaves his seat, shouts out answers and loses a jumper a week. Homework takes three hours and ends in tears.
Adult, 34. Has changed jobs six times. Starts projects with energy, then leaves them unfinished. Has bills in a pile she cannot face. Her mind "never switches off", and she has always been like this.

ADHD is heterogeneous: it looks different from one person to the next, from one setting to another, and over a lifetime. There is no blood test, scan or computer test that makes the diagnosis. It is a clinical diagnosis, made from a careful history of the whole life, information from someone who knew the person as a child, and observation NICE NG87 1.3.2.

What ADHD is not. It is not a lack of intelligence, poor parenting or laziness. It is also not "everyone is a bit ADHD": the diagnosis needs many symptoms, a long history, and clear harm to the person's life.

The 18 symptoms

DSM-5-TR lists 9 inattention symptoms (criterion A1) and 9 hyperactivity-impulsivity symptoms (A2). Pick a domain, then a symptom: each is paraphrased in plain words, with child and adult examples, when it counts, and what to ask. Learn them well: every assessment and rating scale in this course is built on them.

Symptoms are paraphrased from DSM-5-TR (APA 2022), not quoted. Examples are illustrative.

A symptom only counts if it has lasted at least 6 months, happens often, and is clearly more than expected for the person's developmental age. It must not be only defiance, hostility or failure to understand what was asked. Section C (Criteria & Does it count?) has 60 practice scenarios on exactly this.

The diagnostic rules (DSM-5-TR)

DSM-5-TR (2022) is the current edition. Its ADHD criteria are essentially the same as DSM-5 (2013); the accompanying text was updated. All five criteria, A to E, must be met.

The UK threshold is stricter. NICE NG87 1.3.3 says ADHD should be diagnosed only when the symptoms meet DSM-5 or ICD criteria and cause at least moderate psychological, social, educational or occupational impairment, and are pervasive (present in two or more important settings). Someone can meet DSM criteria with only mild impairment and still not get a diagnosis under NICE. Try this in the interactive model below. NICE NG87 1.3.3

Home

Forgetting tasks and losing belongings: keys, wallet, appointments (A1g, A1i).

Ask: "How often do you lose things you need every day?"

Study or work

Keeping attention and organising tasks: unfinished work, missed deadlines (A1b, A1d, A1e).

Ask: "What do managers or tutors say about your work?"

Relationships

Listening, waiting your turn and impulsivity: interrupting, blurting out (A1c, A2g, A2i).

Ask: "Do friends or your partner say you interrupt or do not listen?"

Watch out: problems in only one setting (for example, only at a stressful job) point to that environment, not ADHD.

How a diagnosis is made

Who can diagnose? A specialist psychiatrist, paediatrician or other appropriately qualified healthcare professional with training and expertise in diagnosing ADHD NICE NG87 1.3.1. In UK services this can include trained nurses and pharmacists working in a specialist team, where local governance allows. Diagnosis must not be made from rating scales or observation alone NICE NG87 1.3.2.

Developmental history

Old school reports, baby books and photos show whether symptoms were there before age 12 (criterion B).

Ask: "What did your school reports say about you?"

Information from others

A parent, partner or sibling can describe childhood and daily life. NICE expects this collateral history wherever possible.

Ask: "Who knew you well as a child and could talk to us?"

Everyday functioning

How symptoms affect work, home, money and relationships. This is how you judge impairment (criterion D and the NICE threshold).

Ask: "What has this cost you: jobs, relationships, money?"

Health and other explanations

Sleep, mood, anxiety, substances, thyroid and other conditions can mimic ADHD (criterion E, Module 8).

Ask: "How do you sleep? Any low mood, alcohol or drug use?"

Try it: does it meet the threshold?

Put lesson 3 into practice. Load each patient in turn and predict the verdict before you look at the checks. Then change one rating at a time and watch what flips.

Presentation is not a fixed type. It describes the current picture and often changes. Many children with combined presentation become predominantly inattentive adults, as visible hyperactivity turns into inner restlessness.

Predominantly inattentive

Attention, organisation and follow-through. Quiet and easily missed, especially in girls and women. Often mistaken for anxiety, depression or "not trying".

Ask: "Do you drift off in conversations or lose track of tasks?"

Predominantly hyperactive-impulsive

Restlessness, high activity and poor impulse control. More common in young children; uncommon on its own in adults.

Ask: "Do you talk over people or act before thinking?"

Combined

Both groups meet the threshold. The most common presentation in children seen in clinics. Note the stack of unfinished task cards and the jiggling foot.

Ask: "Which causes you more trouble: focus, or restlessness?"

Other specified ADHD is used when symptoms cause clear impairment but do not meet full criteria, and you state why (for example, "only 4 inattention symptoms"). Unspecified ADHD is used when you do not state the reason, often because there is not enough information yet (for example, in an emergency setting).

ICD-11 codes (WHO)How the same presentations are coded in ICD-11, 6A05.0 to 6A05.Z

ICD-11 code 6A05 describes the same condition. It gives no fixed symptom count: the clinician judges whether the pattern is persistent (at least 6 months), started during development (typically early to mid-childhood), is clearly outside the normal range for age and intellectual level, and causes significant impact. NICE added ICD-11 terminology to NG87 in January 2023.

Beyond the 18 symptoms

These features are not diagnostic criteria, but they are often what patients come in with, and what bothers them most. Choose one to see what it looks like, what else it could be, and what it means for treatment.

Executive function

Executive functions are the skills that turn intentions into action: planning, starting, holding information in mind (working memory), managing time, and switching between tasks. In ADHD the knowledge is there; the self-management is not.

What patients say

"I know exactly what to do. I just cannot make myself start."

"I set six alarms and still miss things."

"Halfway through a task I forget what the next step was."

Could it be something else?
  • Low mood: no drive for anything, including things they used to enjoy; a change from their usual self
  • Learning difficulty: the problem is understanding the task, not starting or organising it
Ask this

"What happens between deciding to do a task and actually starting it?"

Why it matters for prescribing

These problems make the best treatment goals: specific, personal and measurable. Medication often helps, but skills (external reminders, breaking tasks down) are needed too.

Emotional dysregulation

Low frustration tolerance, irritability, quick anger and fast mood shifts that calm down within minutes to hours. DSM-5-TR lists these as associated features, not criteria. For many adults this is the most distressing part of ADHD.

What patients say

"I go from 0 to 100, and ten minutes later it is over."

"Little things make me snap, then I feel awful."

"I have always been like this, since I was a kid."

Could it be something else?
  • Bipolar disorder: episodes lasting days to weeks, a clear change from the usual self, reduced need for sleep
  • Personality disorder (EUPD/BPD): fear of abandonment, unstable sense of self, chronic emptiness, repeated self-harm
  • Depression: persistently low mood most of the day for 2 weeks or more
Ask this

"How long does the anger last? Has it always been like this, or did it start at a particular time?"

Why it matters for prescribing

Record mood and irritability at baseline. Stimulants often improve it, but irritability as the dose wears off (rebound) is a common side effect, so you need a baseline to compare against.

Time blindness

Difficulty sensing how long things take and how much time is left. The future feels unreal until it is urgent, so tasks are left until the deadline forces action. This is not laziness: urgency is what switches attention on.

What patients say

"I think it will take ten minutes. It takes an hour."

"I am always late, even when I really try."

"I only work when the deadline is tomorrow."

Could it be something else?
  • Anxiety-driven avoidance: the person avoids a task because it frightens them, and is often early or over-prepared elsewhere
  • Depression: slowing and lack of energy, rather than poor sense of time
Ask this

"How do you know when it is time to leave for an appointment?"

Why it matters for prescribing

Expect missed appointments and missed doses. Use reminders, simple once-daily regimens where suitable, and do not discharge someone for missing appointments without thinking about ADHD.

Hyperfocus

Intense, long focus on things that are interesting, new or rewarding, often losing track of time and needs (eating, sleeping). ADHD is a problem of regulating attention, not a lack of it: attention goes where interest is, not where it is needed.

What patients say

"I can game for eight hours but cannot read one page of a letter."

"When I am into something, the house could burn down."

"I forget to eat when I am on a project."

Could it be something else?
  • Autism (special interests): narrow, intense interests that are part of a wider pattern, with routines and distress at change or interruption
  • Hypomania: goal-directed activity with high energy, reduced need for sleep, and a change from the usual self
Ask this

"Can you stop when you need to? What happens when you are interrupted?"

Why it matters for prescribing

"They can focus on games" is not evidence against ADHD. Explain this to patients, families and colleagues, who often use it to dismiss the diagnosis.

Sleep problems

Many people with ADHD fall asleep late (a "night owl" body clock), find it hard to wind down because the mind is busy, and struggle to wake. Poor sleep also worsens attention, so the two feed each other.

What patients say

"My brain switches on at 11 pm."

"I cannot get up for work, however early I go to bed."

"I am exhausted all day but wide awake at night."

Could it be something else?
  • Obstructive sleep apnoea: loud snoring, pauses in breathing, daytime sleepiness; more likely with obesity
  • Insomnia from anxiety or depression: worry or low mood keeps them awake
  • Restless legs: an urge to move the legs at night that eases with movement
Ask this

"What time do you fall asleep on days when you do not have to get up? Do you snore?"

Why it matters for prescribing

Record sleep before any medicine. Insomnia is a common stimulant side effect; without a baseline you cannot tell whether the medicine caused it. Treat sleep disorders that mimic ADHD first.

Strengths

Many people describe energy, creativity, curiosity, quick thinking, and doing well in a crisis or a fast-moving job. These are not universal, and the evidence is mostly from people’s own accounts, but naming them builds trust and self-esteem.

What patients say

"I am the one people call in an emergency."

"I have a hundred ideas a day."

"I love jobs where every day is different."

Could it be something else?
  • Being dismissed: strengths do not rule out impairment; many people are successful and still struggling badly
  • Masking: high achievers may be hiding a huge effort to cope
Ask this

"What are you good at? When do things go well for you?"

Why it matters for prescribing

Use strengths in psychoeducation and goal-setting. A strengths-based conversation improves engagement, and diagnosis should never feel like a list of failures. Clinical practice.

Clinical pearl. Ask about all of these at the first appointment. They are often the person’s real reason for coming, they make the best treatment goals, and they give you a baseline for judging benefit and side effects later.

What untreated ADHD costs

ADHD is not "just" a concentration problem. Over a lifetime it affects almost every area of life. Each card shows the cost, and what the evidence says about treatment.

Education

Without treatmentLower grades, more exclusions and dropping out, and leaving school with fewer qualifications than ability would predict.

What treatment evidence showsIn Swedish registry data, people scored higher on university entrance tests during periods on ADHD medication than off it.

Lu et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2017

Work and money

Without treatmentMore job changes, unemployment and underemployment; problems with bills, debt and paperwork.

What treatment evidence showsFew trials measure work outcomes directly. Workplace adjustments and support are part of NICE-recommended care.

Clinical practice; NICE NG87

Injuries and driving

Without treatmentMore accidents, emergency visits and road traffic crashes. Accidents drive the doubled mortality.

What treatment evidence showsSerious road crashes were less likely during months on ADHD medication than off it, in the same people.

Chang et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2017; Dalsgaard 2015

Substance use

Without treatmentHigher rates of smoking, alcohol and drug misuse, often starting younger.

What treatment evidence showsTreating ADHD with stimulants is not linked to more later substance misuse; substance-related events were less likely during medicated periods.

Quinn et al., Am J Psychiatry 2017

Justice system

Without treatmentMore arrests and convictions, often linked to impulsivity.

What treatment evidence showsConvictions were about a third lower in men during periods on medication than off it, and lower in women too.

Lichtenstein et al., NEJM 2012

Relationships and mental health

Without treatmentMore relationship breakdown, low self-esteem, depression and anxiety, often after years of feeling a failure.

What treatment evidence showsDiagnosis and psychoeducation alone often bring relief ("it was not my fault"). Coexisting conditions need their own treatment.

Clinical practice; NICE NG87

How to read this evidence. These long-term studies are observational: they compare the same people during periods on and off medication. That removes many differences between people, but it still shows association, not proof of cause. Short-term randomised trials give the strongest evidence for symptom benefit. A good specialist explains both honestly and never over-promises.
The same person across lifeA picture walkthrough of how ADHD looks from childhood to adulthood

Childhood

Visible fidgeting and attention difficulties. Adults (parents, teachers) provide structure, which can hide how severe the problem is.

Ask: "What did teachers say? Did you need more help than your siblings?"

Adolescence

Planning and organisational demands rise sharply (exams, independent study). Many people first struggle here, especially bright students who coped at primary school.

Ask: "When did school start to feel much harder?"

Adulthood

Restlessness becomes inner (a mind that will not switch off), and the problems shift to work, money, driving and relationships. Fewer symptoms are needed for diagnosis from age 17.

Ask: "Have you always been like this, or did it start recently?"

A changing presentation does not tell the whole story. A "late" first presentation in adulthood is usually lifelong ADHD that was compensated for until demands rose. Truly new symptoms in adulthood need other causes ruled out first (Module 8).

In the UK, diagnosis and prescribing have risen sharply over the last decade, and waiting lists are long. A good specialist neither dismisses ADHD nor over-diagnoses it: they assess carefully and explain the evidence honestly.

Who gets missed

NICE NG87 1.2.1 asks professionals to be aware that ADHD may be more common in:

  • Born preterm
  • Looked-after childrenand young people
  • ODD or conduct disorderchildren and young people
  • Mood disorderssuch as anxiety or depression, in children and young people
  • Family historya close relative with ADHD
  • Epilepsy
  • Other neurodevelopmental conditionsautism, tics, intellectual disability, specific learning difficulties
  • Adult mental health conditions
  • Substance misusecurrent or past
  • Criminal justice contactyouth or adult system
  • Acquired brain injurycheck timing of onset

Girls and women are under-recognised: they are less likely to be referred, more likely to have undiagnosed ADHD, and more likely to be given another diagnosis instead, such as anxiety, depression or personality disorder NICE NG87 1.2.2. Inattention and masking (working hard to hide difficulties) make ADHD less visible. Section D (Special presentations) covers this in depth.

NICE also says do not run universal screening for ADHD in nurseries or schools NICE NG87 1.2.3.

Deep dive: debates a specialist should be able to discuss
  • Relative age effect: the youngest children in a school year are more likely to be diagnosed and treated. Some normal immaturity may be labelled ADHD, so always compare with same-age peers.
  • Private diagnoses and shared care: assessment quality varies, so some NHS areas review private diagnoses before agreeing shared care (Module 6).
  • Long-term benefit: short-term trial evidence for medication is strong. Long-term outcome data are mostly observational.
  • Late-onset ADHD: adults with no childhood history need careful exclusion of other causes, such as substance use, mood or anxiety disorders, sleep disorders and brain injury. Do not diagnose ADHD until these are ruled out.

Recap and practise

5 things to remember

  1. ADHD is more, lifelong and costly. Many symptoms, since childhood, causing real harm. No test diagnoses it: the history does.
  2. Know the 18 symptoms. 9 inattention (A1) and 9 hyperactivity-impulsivity (A2). You need 6 in a domain, or 5 from age 17.
  3. All five criteria must be met. Symptoms (A), onset before 12 (B), 2+ settings (C), impairment (D), not better explained (E).
  4. NICE is stricter. At least moderate impairment, pervasive across 2+ important settings.
  5. Think of who gets missed. Girls and women, and the NG87 1.2.1 groups. Untreated ADHD has real costs, including twice the mortality.

ADHD is a brain-based condition. Genes explain most of the risk, and differences in how brain networks use two chemical messengers, dopamine and noradrenaline, explain many symptoms. Every licensed ADHD medicine works on these messengers. This module explains how, in plain words, so you can predict how each medicine behaves and explain it to patients.

What causes ADHD

In plain words: ADHD is mostly inherited. Many genes each add a tiny amount of risk, and some events before or after birth add more. It is not caused by parenting, sugar or laziness.

~74%of the difference between people in ADHD traits is explained by genes (heritability)
27gene regions linked to ADHD so far; each has a tiny effect. There is no single "ADHD gene" and no genetic test.
~3 yrslater peak in cortical thickness in children with ADHD: brain development is delayed, not abnormal

Things that add risk

Genes are the main cause. These factors add risk on top, and the evidence for each is not equally strong.

Clear link
  • Preterm birth and low birth weightThe earlier the birth, the higher the risk. NICE lists preterm birth as a high-prevalence group.
  • Alcohol in pregnancyFetal alcohol spectrum disorder often includes attention and impulse problems.
  • Severe early deprivationFor example, long institutional care in early childhood.
Likely, check the details
  • Acquired brain injuryAsk when symptoms started: before or after the injury.
  • Toxins such as leadChildhood exposure is linked to attention problems.
Linked, but mostly shared genes
  • Smoking in pregnancySibling studies suggest the link is largely explained by genes the family shares, not the smoking itself.

Myths and facts

MythBad parenting causes ADHD.Parenting does not cause ADHD. A calm, structured home helps every child with it, and parent training is offered as support, not blame.
MythSugar makes children hyperactive.Controlled trials found no effect of sugar on behaviour or attention (Wolraich 1995).
Not shownScreens cause ADHD.Heavy screen use and ADHD go together, but that may be because ADHD draws people to screens. Cause has not been shown.
FactADHD runs in families.About 74% heritable. A parent or sibling with ADHD is one of the strongest clues.
MythThey just need to try harder.The problem is regulating effort and attention, not wanting to succeed. Many people try much harder than others.
PartlyAdults grow out of it.Most improve, but about 65% still have impairing symptoms at 25 (Faraone 2006).
How to explain it to a patient

"ADHD runs in families. Your brain develops and uses its chemical messengers a little differently. It is nobody’s fault, and it is very treatable."

The ADHD brain

Brain scans of large groups show that four networks work a little differently in ADHD. Think of them as teams that must hand over to each other smoothly.

Executive control networkPrefrontal cortex, at the front

What it doesPlans, holds information in mind and puts the brakes on impulses.

In ADHDWeaker control: forgetting what you were doing, acting before thinking.

Reward networkStriatum, deep in the brain

What it doesDecides what is worth doing now, using dopamine.

In ADHDPrefers small rewards now over bigger rewards later; boring tasks feel impossible.

Default mode networkSpread across the midline

What it doesDaydreaming mode. It should switch off when a task starts.

In ADHDDoes not switch off well, so the mind wanders into the task.

Timing networkCerebellum, at the back

What it doesHelps with timing and the smooth flow of movement and thought.

In ADHDProblems judging time; "time blindness".

A scan cannot diagnose ADHD. These are average differences between large groups. They overlap heavily with people without ADHD, so no scan, blood test or brain biomarker can diagnose an individual. Diagnosis stays clinical.

Dopamine and noradrenaline: what they do

Brain cells pass signals with chemical messengers. Two of them, dopamine and noradrenaline (together called catecholamines), tune the prefrontal cortex, the part of the brain that plans, focuses and puts the brakes on. Every licensed ADHD medicine works by boosting them.

Signal and noise: the key idea

Think of the prefrontal cortex as a radio trying to stay on the right station.

Noradrenaline turns up the signal

Through α2A receptors it strengthens the connections between prefrontal cells that hold the task in mind, so the right "station" comes through clearly.

Dopamine turns down the noise

Through D1 receptors it quietens irrelevant inputs, so distractions do not break in.

When either is too weak, the signal fades and the noise wins: the person drifts off, forgets the plan or reacts to every distraction. Both systems work best at a moderate level, which is the inverted U you will try in lesson 6.

What is different in ADHD?

  • Weaker signalling, not "empty" brainsThe circuits that use these messengers work less efficiently on average. It is not simply "low dopamine".
  • Reward feels differentBrain-imaging (PET) studies in adults found fewer dopamine receptors and transporters in the reward pathway (Volkow 2009), which fits with low motivation for slow, boring rewards.
  • Urgency and novelty helpDeadlines, novelty and interest all release catecholamines. That is why people with ADHD often only "switch on" at the last minute.
  • Stress makes it worseStress floods the prefrontal cortex with catecholamines and pushes it past the peak, so performance falls apart under pressure.
Avoid the "low dopamine" myth. Telling patients "you just lack dopamine" is too simple and can suggest they should seek stimulation. Say instead: "the messengers that help your brain focus and filter work less efficiently, and the medicine helps them work better."

The key players: transporters and receptors

To understand any ADHD medicine you need to know six "players" at the synapse. Each medicine acts on one or more of them, and that explains how it helps and why it causes the side effects it does. Choose a player.

The synapse

The tiny gap between two nerve cells. One cell releases a chemical messenger into the gap; the next cell picks it up.

Where it is

Everywhere in the brain, and on the nerves that control the heart, blood vessels and gut.

Its job

Passes signals on. How strong and how long the signal lasts decides whether the message gets through clearly.

What ADHD medicines do to it

Every ADHD medicine acts here. None of them adds anything new to the brain: they change how strongly and how long the brain’s own messengers work.

What that means for the patient

This is a useful reassurance: the medicine does not change who you are, it helps your own signals work better.

VMAT2

Vesicular MonoAmine Transporter 2: a pump on the wall of the tiny storage bubbles (vesicles) inside the nerve ending.

Where it is

Inside dopamine and noradrenaline nerve endings (and serotonin ones).

Its job

Packs dopamine and noradrenaline into vesicles, ready to be released when the nerve fires.

What ADHD medicines do to it

Amfetamines disrupt VMAT2, so messenger leaks out of the vesicles into the cell and is then pushed out into the gap. Methylphenidate, atomoxetine and guanfacine do not act here.

What that means for the patient

This is why amfetamines give a bigger rise in dopamine than methylphenidate, and why immediate-release dexamfetamine has the highest misuse potential.

Everyday picturePacking letters into envelopes, ready to post.

DAT

The dopamine transporter (DA = dopamine, T = transporter): a protein pump in the wall of the nerve ending.

Where it is

Dense in the striatum and the nucleus accumbens (the reward centre). There are very few in the prefrontal cortex.

Its job

Sucks dopamine back out of the gap into the nerve ending within milliseconds, which ends the signal. The dopamine is then reused.

What ADHD medicines do to it

Methylphenidate blocks it (a plug in the pump). Amfetamines ride in through it and run it backwards, pushing dopamine out. Atomoxetine and guanfacine do not act on it.

What that means for the patient

Blocking DAT in the reward centre gives a fast dopamine rise, which explains the misuse potential and controlled drug status of stimulants. Dopamine in the striatum also explains why tics can appear or worsen.

Everyday pictureA recycling vacuum that clears the messenger away. Block the vacuum and the message stays longer.

NET

The norepinephrine transporter (norepinephrine is the American name for noradrenaline): the same kind of pump, for noradrenaline.

Where it is

On noradrenaline nerve endings across the whole cortex, including the prefrontal cortex, and on the nerves that control the heart and blood vessels.

Its job

Clears noradrenaline from the gap. In the prefrontal cortex, where there are few DAT pumps, NET also clears most of the dopamine.

What ADHD medicines do to it

Methylphenidate blocks it, amfetamines reverse it, and atomoxetine blocks only NET.

What that means for the patient

More noradrenaline at the heart and vessels raises pulse and blood pressure, so these are monitored with stimulants and atomoxetine. Because atomoxetine blocks only NET, it raises dopamine in the prefrontal cortex but not in the reward centre, so it is not a controlled drug.

Everyday pictureA second vacuum, for a different messenger, that also tidies up dopamine in the planning part of the brain.

D1 receptor

A receptor on the receiving cell. When dopamine docks, it changes how that cell behaves.

Where it is

On prefrontal cortex cells (D1). In the striatum, D2 receptors matter more.

Its job

Turns down the noise: helps the prefrontal cortex ignore irrelevant inputs and distractions. It works best at a moderate level; too much switches the prefrontal cortex off (the inverted U).

What ADHD medicines do to it

No ADHD medicine docks on D1 directly. Stimulants and atomoxetine raise the dopamine that reaches it.

What that means for the patient

Explains why the right dose matters: too little and distractions win; too much and the person becomes flat, rigid or irritable.

Everyday pictureA letterbox: the message only counts when it is delivered through the right door.

α2A receptor

A receptor for noradrenaline that responds to quite low levels (it is "high affinity").

Where it is

On the tiny spines of prefrontal cortex cells, and in the brainstem, where it calms the body’s fight-or-flight system.

Its job

In the prefrontal cortex it turns up the signal, helping cells hold information in mind. In the brainstem it lowers pulse and blood pressure.

What ADHD medicines do to it

Guanfacine docks on it directly. Stimulants and atomoxetine raise the noradrenaline that reaches it.

What that means for the patient

Explains guanfacine’s side effects: sleepiness, low blood pressure and a slow pulse. It also explains why guanfacine must be tapered: stopping suddenly can cause rebound high blood pressure.

The whole story in four lines

Vesicles (filled by VMAT2) release dopamine and noradrenaline into the synapse. They dock on receptors (D1, α2A) to pass the message on. Recycling pumps (DAT, NET) clear them away to end the signal. ADHD medicines keep more messenger at the receptors for longer, or act on the receptor directly.

Four routes to the same goal

Every licensed ADHD medicine boosts dopamine and/or noradrenaline signalling in the prefrontal cortex, but by a different route. That route explains how fast it works, how long it lasts and its side effects.

Methylphenidate

Blocks the recycling pumps (DAT and NET), so messengers stay longer

Dexamfetamine and lisdexamfetamine

Push messengers out and also block recycling

Atomoxetine

Blocks only the noradrenaline pump (NET)

Guanfacine

Acts like noradrenaline on α2A receptors in the prefrontal cortex

Same messengers, outside the attention circuit

The medicines cannot target only the prefrontal cortex. The same messengers work all over the brain and body, so the side effects are predictable from the mechanism. This is why NICE asks you to monitor pulse, blood pressure, weight, sleep and mood.

Heart and blood vessels

WhyNoradrenaline drives heart rate and blood pressure.

What you see and doRaised pulse and blood pressure. Measure before starting and after every dose change.

Appetite

WhyCatecholamines in the hypothalamus reduce hunger.

What you see and doReduced appetite and weight loss. Monitor weight (and height in children); eat a good breakfast before the dose.

Sleep and arousal

WhyBoth messengers keep the brain awake and alert.

What you see and doDifficulty falling asleep, especially if the dose lasts into the evening. Record sleep at baseline.

Movement

WhyDopamine in the striatum controls movement and habits.

What you see and doTics can appear or worsen. Ask at every review.

Reward

WhyFast dopamine rises in the nucleus accumbens feel rewarding.

What you see and doMisuse and diversion potential, highest with immediate-release stimulants. That is why stimulants are controlled drugs.

Mood

WhyToo much catecholamine pushes the brain past the peak.

What you see and doIrritability, anxiety, a "flat" feeling; rarely psychosis or mania. Think "dose too high".

Try it: finding the right dose

More is not always better. The prefrontal cortex works best with a moderate level of dopamine and noradrenaline. This "inverted U" explains why we titrate slowly, why too high a dose makes people worse, and why stress can make symptoms flare.

Clinical pearl. If someone becomes irritable, flat or anxious after a dose increase, think "past the peak" before "needs more". Reduce, do not escalate, and discuss with the prescriber.

  1. Release. An action potential makes vesicles fuse with the terminal membrane and release dopamine into the synaptic cleft. Nearby noradrenergic terminals release noradrenaline in the same way.
  2. Reuptake ends the signal. The dopamine transporter (DAT) and noradrenaline transporter (NET) pump transmitter back into the terminal within milliseconds, so each signal is brief. VMAT2 then refills the vesicles.
  3. Methylphenidate blocks DAT and NET. Methylphenidate binds DAT and NET and blocks reuptake (the red diamonds). The SPC says it is "thought to block the reuptake of noradrenaline and dopamine into the presynaptic neurone and increase the release of these monoamines into the extraneuronal space" (Concerta XL SPC §5.1). "Increase the release" here means more transmitter outside the neuron. Unlike amfetamine, methylphenidate is not a transporter substrate and does not empty vesicles, so it is best taught as mainly a reuptake inhibitor.
  4. More signal at the receptors. Dopamine and noradrenaline stay longer in the cleft and stimulate D1 and α-adrenergic receptors. This is thought to improve signal-to-noise in prefrontal–striatal circuits, and so attention and impulse control.

In clinic

What the patient notices

The effect switches on within about an hour and off as blood levels fall. That on–off pattern is predictable, and the time it wears off is the main thing that differs between brands.

Side effects the mechanism explains

Raised pulse and blood pressure, reduced appetite, trouble getting to sleep if cover runs late, and irritability as it wears off ("rebound").

Practical point

Few drug interactions because it is not cleared by CYP enzymes; never combine with an MAOI. If cover ends too early, the answer is usually a different formulation or timing, not a bigger dose.

  1. Normal release and reuptake. As in any catecholamine synapse, vesicles release dopamine and DAT/NET recycle it.
  2. Amfetamine is carried in by DAT. Dexamfetamine is a substrate of DAT and NET, so the transporter carries it into the terminal. Lisdexamfetamine itself is inactive; it is hydrolysed, mainly by red blood cells, to dexamfetamine (Elvanse SPC §5.1).
  3. VMAT2 is disrupted. Inside the terminal, amfetamine interferes with VMAT2 and vesicle storage, so dopamine leaks out of vesicles into the cytosol.
  4. Transporters run in reverse. The high cytosolic dopamine drives DAT (and NET) in reverse, pumping transmitter out into the cleft independently of nerve firing. Amfetamine also competitively blocks reuptake. It also activates the intracellular trace-amine receptor TAAR1, which promotes transporter reversal and internalisation, and it weakly inhibits MAO, so less cytosolic dopamine is broken down (simplified textbook model; not in the SPC).
  5. Large rise in synaptic dopamine and noradrenaline. The net result is a larger increase in released transmitter than with a pure reuptake blocker. This explains the strong effect, and the higher misuse potential of IR dexamfetamine.

In clinic

What the patient notices

A stronger effect per milligram than methylphenidate. Lisdexamfetamine rises and falls smoothly, so many people notice less of a sharp "wear-off".

Side effects the mechanism explains

The same catecholamine set: raised pulse and blood pressure, reduced appetite and weight, insomnia, irritability. Misuse potential is highest with immediate-release dexamfetamine; lower, but not absent, with lisdexamfetamine.

Practical point

Acidic urine (for example large doses of vitamin C) speeds excretion and shortens the effect; alkaline urine (for example some antacids) prolongs it. Never combine with an MAOI. Amfetamine is a "specified drug" under UK drug-driving law: prescribed use is defensible if taken as directed and driving is not impaired.

  1. Noradrenaline is released. Noradrenergic terminals in the prefrontal cortex release noradrenaline (NA) onto α2A and other adrenergic receptors.
  2. NET clears NA — and dopamine in the PFC. NET recycles NA. The prefrontal cortex has few dopamine transporters, so NET also clears much of the dopamine there.
  3. Atomoxetine blocks NET. Atomoxetine is "a highly selective and potent inhibitor of the pre-synaptic noradrenaline transporter", with no direct effect on serotonin or dopamine transporters (atomoxetine SPC §5.1).
  4. More NA everywhere; more DA mainly in the PFC. Noradrenaline rises wherever NET is blocked, and dopamine rises mainly in the prefrontal cortex. It does not rise much in the striatum and nucleus accumbens, which is thought to explain atomoxetine's low misuse potential.

In clinic

What the patient notices

No "on–off" effect: benefit builds gradually over several weeks, so early reviews often show little change. Nausea and tiredness are common early on and often settle.

Side effects the mechanism explains

Nausea, reduced appetite, sleepiness or insomnia, raised pulse and blood pressure. Warnings: suicidal thoughts (especially early in treatment in young people), rare liver injury, and sexual dysfunction in adults.

Practical point

Allow an adequate trial before judging it. Fluoxetine and paroxetine (strong CYP2D6 inhibitors) and genetic "poor metabolisers" can have several-fold higher levels, so titrate more slowly. Useful when misuse or diversion is a worry.

6Guanfacine — α2A-adrenoceptor agonist on prefrontal dendritic spines

  • Glutamate
  • cAMP
  • HCN channel
  • α2A receptor

Use Next, or tap a number on the diagram

  1. Glutamate input arrives. A pyramidal neuron in the prefrontal cortex receives glutamate signals from neighbouring neurons on its dendritic spines. Recurrent firing in these networks holds information "online" (working memory).
  2. cAMP opens HCN channels and the signal leaks. When cAMP is high (for example under stress or with low noradrenergic tone), HCN channels near the synapse open. Current leaks out and the network signal fades.
  3. Guanfacine stimulates α2A receptors. Guanfacine is a selective α2A agonist, with 15–20 times higher affinity for α2A than for α2B/α2C (Intuniv SPC §5.1). α2A couples to Gi, which inhibits adenylyl cyclase (AC), so cAMP falls.
  4. HCN channels close and the signal persists. With less cAMP, HCN channels close, the input is retained and prefrontal network firing strengthens. This is the proposed basis for improved attention and behavioural control. It is a postsynaptic effect, not a change in dopamine release.

In clinic

What the patient notices

Sleepiness is common in the first weeks of treatment and often eases. Benefit builds gradually; there is no wear-off in the afternoon.

Side effects the mechanism explains

Sleepiness, low blood pressure, slow pulse, dizziness on standing, weight gain. These come from the same α2A action lowering sympathetic outflow from the brainstem.

Practical point

Check pulse and blood pressure before and during titration. Never stop suddenly: taper by no more than 1 mg every 3–7 days to avoid rebound high blood pressure. Halve the dose with moderate or strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (Intuniv SPC). Licensed in the UK for ages 6–17.

Deep dive: pharmacokinetics that matter in practice
  • Methylphenidate: short half-life (~2–3 h); formulations (IR, OROS, beaded MR) determine profile. Metabolised mainly by carboxylesterase CES1 — not CYP-dependent.
  • Lisdexamfetamine: conversion is rate-limited, giving low between-patient variability; not affected by GI pH in the way amfetamine salts are.
  • Amfetamines: renal excretion increases in acidic urine (e.g. high-dose vitamin C) and decreases in alkaline urine (e.g. sodium bicarbonate) {SPC}.
  • Atomoxetine: CYP2D6 — poor metabolisers (and those taking strong CYP2D6 inhibitors such as fluoxetine or paroxetine) have markedly higher exposure (SPC: ~6–8-fold AUC with strong inhibitors at steady state).
  • Guanfacine: CYP3A4 — halve the dose with moderate/strong CYP3A4 inhibitors; increase with inducers {SPC}.
How to explain it to a patient

"Your brain uses two messengers, dopamine and noradrenaline, to focus and to put the brakes on. In ADHD the signal is a bit weak. This medicine helps the signal stay strong for a few hours, like turning up the volume on the part of your brain that plans and concentrates. It does not change who you are, and we will find the dose that works for you step by step."

Recap and practise

5 things to remember

  1. ADHD is mostly genetic. About 74% heritable, many genes of tiny effect. Not caused by parenting or sugar.
  2. Networks, not one spot. Executive control, reward, default mode and timing networks differ on average. No scan can diagnose it.
  3. Two messengers. Dopamine ("worth doing") and noradrenaline ("pay attention") tune the prefrontal cortex.
  4. The inverted U. Too little and too much both impair performance, so titrate carefully and reduce if the person gets worse.
  5. Mechanism predicts practice. Reuptake block or release (stimulants, fast, CD) vs NET block or α2A agonism (non-stimulants, slow, not CD).

Recognition and referral: children and young people

Key idea

For children the route is staged: support starts early, and only those who still struggle are referred. Severe problems skip the wait.

1
Step 1

A concern is raised

A parent, a teacher or the young person notices a pattern.

DoListen to the concern and ask school what they see. When a child with disordered conduct is referred to the school SENCO, the SENCO should also tell parents about local parent-training programmes.

Don’tScreen whole nurseries or schools for ADHD. NICE does not support universal screening.

2
Step 2

Primary care judges the impact

Three questions decide what happens next.

  • How severe?How bad are the behaviour and attention problems?
  • Who is affected?How do they affect the child or young person, and the parents or carers?
  • Where?How far do they reach across home, school and other settings?
3
Step 3

Support first

If the problems are affecting development or family life, support comes before referral.

  • Watchful waitingFor up to 10 weeks, with school and home strategies.
  • Parent supportOffer a referral to group-based, ADHD-focused support. It should not wait for a diagnosis.

Severe impairment?Skip the wait. Refer directly to secondary care: a child psychiatrist, paediatrician or specialist ADHD CAMHS.

4
Step 4

After support, how are things?

Review the child after the support period.

ImprovingContinue support in primary care. Keep using school and parenting strategies; no referral needed. Review if things change.

Still at least moderate impairmentRefer to secondary care for a full assessment: a child psychiatrist, paediatrician or specialist ADHD CAMHS. Whoever refers should tell the GP.

!
The rule

What primary care must not do

Primary care should not make the initial diagnosis or start medication in children or young people with suspected ADHD.

"Only a specialist can diagnose ADHD, after a full assessment. The first step is your GP, and it helps to bring what school has said. Parenting support groups are available now, and you do not need a diagnosis to use them."

Recognition and referral: adults

Key idea

Adults often come forward after a child is diagnosed, a crisis at work or in a relationship, or after reading about ADHD. NICE sets out who to refer, and every assessment starts with why now.

1
Route 1

No childhood diagnosis

Refer to a mental health specialist trained in ADHD when all four are true.

  1. Began in childhoodTypical symptoms of ADHD that started when they were young.
  2. Persisted throughout lifeNot a recent change.
  3. Not explained by another psychiatric diagnosisOther conditions may coexist.
  4. At least moderate impairmentPsychological, social, educational or occupational.
2
Other routes

Also refer, or think again

Two more groups to refer, and two warning signs.

Treated as a child, symptoms continuingRefer to general adult psychiatric services for assessment, if at least moderate impairment.

Moving from children’s servicesReassess at school-leaving age; if treatment is still needed, plan a smooth transition, usually complete by 18.

Symptoms began recentlyNew adult-onset problems need other causes ruled out first: mood, anxiety, sleep, substances, brain injury, menopause.

Only in one settingProblems confined to one job or one relationship point to that setting, not ADHD.

3
Route 2

Already diagnosed as a child?

Check the old diagnosis, then assess what matters now.

Many adults were diagnosed or treated as children and come back later, often at a life change (university, a new job, a baby) when the structure that helped them falls away. First get the evidence: with consent, ask for the original diagnostic report and the CAMHS or paediatric letters, plus the treatment history: which medicines, what doses, what helped, side effects, and why treatment stopped.

A full assessment and a clear diagnosis on fileYou do not start from zero: the records are your evidence of childhood ADHD (criterion B).

Then assess what matters nowCurrent symptoms, impairment, coexisting conditions, and whether treatment is still needed.

No report, or a doubtful diagnosisNo report can be found, the diagnosis was made without a full assessment, or the picture does not fit ADHD.

Do a full adult assessmentAs for a new referral, with collateral history and any school reports you can get.

Why check at all? ADHD often eases with age. By 25, about 15% still meet full criteria, but about 65% still have impairing symptoms (Faraone 2006). A childhood diagnosis tells you ADHD was there; it does not tell you what is needed now.

4
Every adult

Why now, and whose idea?

Ask two questions at the start: what made you seek help now, and whose idea was the referral.

The answers shape the whole assessment.

A life eventA child’s diagnosis, a new job or course, a baby, or a crisis at work or in a relationship.

Reading about ADHDMany adults recognise themselves online. Ask what they recognised, then test it like any other history: real examples, childhood, settings and impairment.

Someone else wants the diagnosisA partner, employer, university or parent pushing; exam arrangements, benefits or a court case; or wanting stimulants to study or work longer.

What to doPressure is not a reason to refuse. It means: lean harder on collateral and records, check that answers are consistent, and agree expectations early. A person who did not want the referral may not engage with treatment.

Who diagnoses, and what a full assessment includes

Key idea

ADHD is diagnosed by a trained specialist after a full assessment that includes other people’s accounts and real impairment. A score alone never makes the diagnosis.

1
Part 1

Who diagnoses

Only a specialist with training and expertise in diagnosing ADHD.

Can diagnoseA specialist psychiatrist, paediatrician or other appropriately qualified healthcare professional with ADHD training and expertise. In UK services this can include trained nurses and pharmacists working within a specialist team and its governance.

Should notPrimary care should not make the initial diagnosis or start medication in children or young people with suspected ADHD. Adults without a childhood diagnosis are referred to a specialist.

No single test makes the diagnosis. Not a rating scale, an observation, a scan or a computer test on its own.

2
Part 2

What a full assessment covers

A full clinical and psychosocial assessment, not a questionnaire.

  • Developmental and psychiatric historyPregnancy, birth, early development, school, mental health across life.
  • Reports from people who know themParents, partners, teachers; school reports and records.
  • Mental stateHow the person presents now: mood, anxiety, thought, risk.
  • Needs and coexisting conditionsWhat support is needed, and what else is present (anxiety, autism, substance use, sleep).
  • CircumstancesSocial, family, education or work situation, and physical health.
  • Children: carers and the child’s own viewParents’ or carers’ mental health; the child’s views taken into account wherever possible.

As an option, NICE allows QbTest to help diagnose ADHD in people aged 6 to 17.

3
Part 3

Collateral history

Self-report is limited, especially about childhood. Ask people who knew the person then, and who know them now.

  • Parent or carerEarly childhoodThe best source for symptoms before age 12, development and behaviour at home.
  • School reportsWritten at the timeLook for "daydreams", "easily distracted", "could do better if they concentrated", "talks in class".
  • Partner, sibling or friendDaily life nowHow the person manages time, money, home and relationships today.
  • RecordsObjective historyGP and child health records, previous assessments, employer feedback, driving or justice records.

When informants disagree. A big difference between parent and teacher, or patient and partner, is information, not an error. Ask why: different demands, different relationships, masking, or a problem confined to one setting. Practise it in the Practise box (“Parent vs teacher”) and in section B, step 12.

4
Part 4

Impairment

Impairment is the heart of the threshold: at least moderate, in more than one important setting.

Check every area of life

  • EducationExclusions, repeated years, results below ability, dropping out
  • WorkJob losses, warnings, frequent job changes
  • RelationshipsConflict, breakdown, difficulty keeping friends
  • MoneyDebt, unpaid bills, impulsive spending
  • Driving and injuriesAccidents, speeding fines, A&E visits
  • SubstancesAlcohol, cannabis, stimulant misuse
  • Justice systemArrests, cautions, convictions
  • Self-care and healthMissed appointments, poor sleep, self-esteem

The threshold. Symptoms without at least moderate impairment do not meet NICE’s threshold, however high the score. Children’s and young people’s own views count when judging it.

Rating scales and tests: what each one does

A rating scale is a questionnaire. The person, a parent, a partner or a teacher rates how often each symptom happens (for example 0 = never to 3 = very often). The total is compared with a cut-off or with scores from people of the same age. A test is a task the person does, such as a computer task that records attention and movement.

Both give structure and numbers, but they are adjuncts: helpers to your interview, not the diagnosis. A high score shows that symptoms are reported, not why. Anxiety, low mood and poor sleep can score high too. NICE calls scales such as the Conners and the SDQ valuable adjuncts, but a diagnosis must never rest on them alone. Each tool below does one job:

    • Based on DSM-5-TR · ICD-11 · NICE NG87

    Everyone loses keys and drifts off in meetings. This section teaches you to decide when a behaviour really counts as an ADHD symptom, using the two classification systems side by side and 60 real-life child and adult scenarios.

    By the end of this section you can
    • Compare DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 6A05 element by element
    • Judge when each of the 18 symptoms counts (the symptoms themselves are taught in Module 1)
    • Apply the six questions that decide whether a behaviour counts
    • Record the source and age for each symptom and judge met, not met or insufficient evidence

    Two systems, one pattern

    Key idea

    An ADHD diagnosis is not a score. It is five checks (DSM-5-TR calls them criteria A to E). The person must pass all five. Then you record the presentation and severity.

    DSM-5-TR

    Counts symptoms

    American Psychiatric Association, 2022. 18 listed symptoms, a fixed count and five criteria, A to E. Adds a severity rating.

    ICD-11

    Describes the pattern

    World Health Organization. Same pattern, but no fixed count. Codes the presentation as 6A05.0, .1, .2, .Y or .Z.

    NICE NG87 1.3.3

    Accepts either

    Use DSM-5 or ICD-11, and add one rule: symptoms cause at least moderate impairment in more than one important setting.

    The five gates, one at a time

    Each gate shows what you check, how DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 word it, and a passing and failing example.

    A
    Gate 1

    Symptoms

    Enough symptoms, often enough, for long enough, and more than expected for the person’s age.

    DSM-5-TR

    6 or more of the 9 symptoms in either domain (5 or more at 17 and over), for at least 6 months, inconsistent with developmental level.

    Girls and women: masked ADHD

    ADHD is under-recognised in girls and women. NICE NG87 1.2.2 asks clinicians to be aware that they:

    • Are less likely to be referredfor assessment
    • May be more likely to be undiagnosedinto adulthood
    • May get the wrong diagnosisof another mental health or neurodevelopmental condition

    An international expert consensus on females, led from the UK (Young et al. 2020), describes presentations that are often subtler and more internalised: daydreaming rather than disruption, chattiness rather than running about. Strategies such as lists, alarms and over-preparation mask the symptoms.

    In clinic

    When it counts

    The symptoms are lifelong, present in two or more settings, and the cost is real even if results look fine: lost sleep, distress, collapse when demands rise.

    When it does not

    A recent dip in focus driven by worry, low mood or a life event, with no childhood history.

    Ask

    “How long does homework (or admin) take compared with others?” “What happens if you don’t use your lists and alarms?” “How do you feel at the end of the day?”

    Autism and intellectual disability

    Autism and intellectual disability do not rule out ADHD. They change how you judge each symptom. Work through the four steps.

    1
    The rule

    Both can be diagnosed together

    Autism or an intellectual disability does not rule ADHD out. A person can have both.

    What changes is how you judge the symptoms. Each condition can cause problems that look like ADHD, so for every symptom ask:

    • Autism: what is driving it?
    • Intellectual disability: is it more than expected for the person's level of ability?

    NICE says not to exclude ADHD just because a person is autistic.

    2
    Autism

    What is driving it?

    Autism and ADHD can both cause poor attention, restlessness and social difficulty. The cause is what tells them apart.

    More like ADHD
    • Attention drifts in most tasks, including ones the person enjoys.
    • Restless or fidgety most of the time, in calm places too.
    • Interrupts or acts without thinking, then regrets it.
    More like autism
    • Attention is deep for their own interests and switches off for everything else.
    • Restless mainly with noise, crowds, bright light or a change in routine.
    • Social difficulty comes from not reading what others mean or expect.

    Points to ADHD as wellTom, 30, is autistic. At work, at home and even in his favourite hobby he drifts off, misses details and forgets appointments. He has been like this since primary school.

    Autism alone explains itHe only loses focus on tasks that do not interest him, or in noisy, busy rooms.

    Illustrative example, not a real patient.

    3
    Intellectual disability

    Compare with the same level of ability

    Judge each symptom against people with a similar level of ability, not people of the same age.

    Attention and self-control develop with ability, not just age. An adult with an intellectual disability may manage like a much younger person. That alone is not ADHD.

    Worked example: Sian, 24, has a mild intellectual disability and goes to a day centre. Staff and family see that:

    • she cannot stay with a 5-minute task that others at the centre, with similar abilities, finish;
    • she loses her belongings every day;
    • she interrupts all the time, in groups and one to one;
    • her mother says she has been like this since primary school.

    This countsPeople with similar abilities manage the task, so her disability alone does not explain it. It happens at the centre and at home, and has been there since childhood.

    It would not count ifshe only struggled with tasks too hard for her level of ability, only in one place, or only since a recent change (a new home, staff or medicine, or pain or distress she cannot put into words). Look for that cause first.

    How to adapt the assessmentInterview, communication and informants
    • Use an interview made for adults with an intellectual disability: DIVA-5 ID from the DIVA Foundation.
    • Use pictures and simple words, and keep sessions short.
    • Ask carers and staff who know the person well, in more than one setting.
    • Watch the person in familiar places, not only in the clinic.

    Illustrative example, not a real patient.

    4
    Treatment

    Same medicines, slower and closer

    Offer the same ADHD medicines as anyone else, but increase the dose more slowly and check more often.

    • Same choices. Being autistic or having an intellectual disability does not change which medicines are offered.
    • Smaller, slower steps. Raise the dose in smaller steps, or wait longer between steps.
    • Check more often. Some people cannot easily say how they feel. Ask carers about sleep, appetite, mood and behaviour, and look for changes.

    Older adults

    ADHD can be diagnosed at any age, but in older adults one question comes first: is this lifelong ADHD, or something new? Work through the four steps.

    1
    The key question

    Lifelong ADHD, or something new?

    ADHD starts in childhood. Attention problems that are new in later life usually have another cause.

    Points to lifelong ADHD
    • The problems go back to school days.
    • The same pattern runs through work, money, relationships and home.
    • Someone who knew them young describes it too.
    • They have always leaned on coping tricks: lists, alarms, a partner who organises.
    Points to something new
    • The problems started in the last few years.
    • They are getting steadily worse.
    • New memory loss: forgetting recent conversations, getting lost in familiar places.
    • A clear trigger: an illness, a new medicine, poor sleep or a bereavement.

    Assess for ADHDMargaret, 67, has always been late, disorganised and losing things. Her sister says she was the same at school. She coped with lists and a flexible job.

    Look for another cause firstJohn, 70, was organised all his working life. Over the past two years he forgets conversations and gets lost driving. He needs a memory assessment, not an ADHD assessment.

    Illustrative examples, not real patients. NICE asks clinicians to consider ADHD at every age, with the criteria adjusted for age.

    2
    Evidence

    Find proof of the lifelong pattern

    An older person's memory of childhood can be patchy, so evidence from other people and old records matters even more.

    Good sources of evidence:

    • Brothers, sisters or a partner who have known them for a long time.
    • Old school reports: comments such as "daydreams", "careless", "could do better if they tried".
    • Work history: many job changes, warnings, appraisals about organisation or deadlines.
    • Previous records: earlier assessments, GP notes, past treatment for mood or anxiety that never quite helped.

    If nobody who knew them as a child is available, record that, and weigh the rest of the evidence more carefully.

    3
    Rule out

    Check for new causes

    Before calling it ADHD, look for the common causes of new attention and memory problems in later life.

    • Memory and thinking: early dementia or mild cognitive impairment. Losing new memories is not typical of ADHD. Refer for a memory assessment if in doubt.
    • Mood: depression and anxiety both affect concentration.
    • Sleep: sleep apnoea (loud snoring, daytime sleepiness) and poor sleep.
    • Hearing and sight: missing what is said can look like not listening.
    • Medicines: sleeping tablets, strong painkillers and anticholinergic medicines (for example some bladder and allergy medicines) can slow thinking.
    • Physical health: ask the GP about thyroid, anaemia and other checks if anything suggests them.
    4
    Before prescribing

    Specialist decision, careful heart check

    Most ADHD medicines are not licensed or not studied in older adults, so prescribing is a specialist, often off-label, decision.

    • Why off-label? Most SmPCs say the medicine should not be used in the elderly, or that it has not been studied over 60 or 65. Lisdexamfetamine has limited data and asks for a thorough check first.
    • Check the heart carefully. Older adults more often have high blood pressure and heart disease. Do the usual heart questions, blood pressure and pulse, and an ECG or cardiology opinion when the NICE heart features are present.
    • Check other medicines. More medicines mean more interactions, for example with blood pressure tablets.
    • Start low, go slow, and check blood pressure and pulse at every dose change.
    • Record the off-label decision: why, what the person was told, and that they agreed.

    Before any treatment: a shared decision

    Before any treatment starts, drug or non-drug, NICE asks for a proper two-way conversation. The aim is a plan the person understands, agrees with and can keep to. Work through the four steps.

    1
    Step 1

    Set up the conversation

    Before talking about treatment, agree who is in the room and how much the person wants to decide.

    1. A
      Who joins in

      Ask young people and adults if they would like a parent, partner, close friend or carer with them. Another person can help them remember what was said and support them at home.

      You can ask“Would you like anyone to join us when we talk about treatment?”

    2. B
      Hear the child's own view

      Children and young people should say in their own words how they feel, not only through their parents. Talk to them directly and use words they understand.

      You can ask“What is hardest for you at school or at home right now?”

    3. C
      How involved they want to be

      Some people want to make every decision; others want a clear recommendation. Ask, and ask again later: the answer changes as children grow up or move to adult services.

      You can ask“How much do you want to decide yourself, and how much would you like me to guide you?”

    2
    Step 2

    The five things to talk about

    NICE lists five topics to cover before any treatment starts, drug or non-drug.

    1. 1
      Benefits and harms of each option

      Explain what non-drug support and medicine can each do, how well they work, the common side effects, and that some people do not respond to a medicine.

      You can ask“What have you heard about ADHD medicine? Shall I go through what it can and cannot do?”

    2. 2
      A healthy lifestyle

      Exercise, good sleep and regular meals help everyone, and can make ADHD symptoms easier to manage. They support treatment; they do not replace it.

      You can ask“How are your sleep and exercise at the moment?”

    3. 3
      Their preferences and worries

      People are influenced by the news, social media, teachers, family and friends, and some doubt the diagnosis. Listen without judging, answer the worries, and write them in the plan.

      You can ask“Is there anything that worries you about starting treatment?”

    4. 4
      Other conditions

      Anxiety, tics, autism, low mood or drug and alcohol use can change which treatment comes first, and how fast to go.

      You can ask“Is anything else affecting you, such as worry, mood or drinking?”

    5. 5
      Sticking to treatment

      Adherence means taking treatment as agreed. Talk about what could get in the way: taking a dose at school or work, remembering appointments, cost or travel.

      You can ask“What might make it hard to take a tablet every morning or come to reviews?”

    3
    Step 3

    Build one plan

    Write one shared plan that covers the whole of the person's life, not only the symptoms.

    NICE calls this a holistic plan: it covers feelings and behaviour, and school or work, as well as symptoms. When you write it, take into account:

    • How severe things are: the symptoms, the impairment and how they affect everyday life, including sleep.
    • Their goals: what the person wants to change, in their own words (for example "keep my job" or "stop getting detentions").
    • Their strengths: NICE calls these resilience and protective factors. For example a supportive family, a good routine, a job or hobby that suits them.
    • Other conditions: which one is causing the most trouble, and so which to treat first.

    One team: keep the same team, or hand over smoothly, so the person does not have to start again each time. NICE calls this continuity of care.

    4
    Step 4

    Keep the door open

    Tell people they can change their mind about treatment at any time.

    A decision made today is not fixed. Someone may try non-drug support first and ask for medicine later, or stop a medicine and try something else. Say this clearly, record the decision and when it will be reviewed.

    How to explain it to a patient

    Non-drug care at every age

    What non-drug care looks like depends on age. For young children it is the treatment; for older children and adults it sits alongside medicine, or instead of it. Choose an age group.

    1
    Under 5

    Parent training is the treatment

    For children under 5, the main treatment is a group course for their parents or carers. Medicine is rare.

    1. 1
      Group parent training comes first

      A course for parents or carers, focused on ADHD and run by a trained leader. Young children learn how to behave mostly from the adults around them, so the course teaches parents practical tools: praise, clear routines and calm, consistent responses. It does not mean the child has a conduct problem; the same principles simply work well.

      In practiceOffer it as the first treatment, not as a hurdle before medicine.

    2. 2
      If problems remain

      If, after the course and changes at home and nursery, ADHD still causes significant problems in more than one setting, get advice from a specialist ADHD service that knows young children well (ideally a tertiary, regional service).

    3. 3
      Medicine only with a second opinion

      No ADHD medicine is licensed under 5. Do not offer one without a second opinion from a specialist service with expertise in young children.

    2
    Children 5+ and young people

    Support for every family, more when needed

    Every family gets information and support. Extra help is added for defiance, and talking therapy if medicine is not enough on its own.

    1. 1
      Information and support for every family

      Short, ADHD-focused support for parents and carers: it can be a group of just 1 or 2 sessions. It covers what ADHD is and why it happens, practical parenting strategies and, with consent, contact with the school or college. Include both parents or carers if possible.

    2. 2
      Parent training for defiance or conduct problems

      If the child also shows signs of oppositional defiant disorder (persistent anger, arguing, refusing rules) or conduct disorder (aggression, breaking serious rules), offer a full parent-training programme as well. If a group is hard for the family (for example language, disability, illness or transport), offer one-to-one sessions.

    3. 3
      Medicine only when changes have not been enough

      Offer medicine only if ADHD still causes significant problems in at least one area of life after changes at home and school have been made and reviewed, the family has talked through ADHD information, and the pre-treatment checks are done.

      In practiceChanges at school means things like sitting near the teacher, short written instructions and movement breaks.

    4. 4
      CBT if medicine helps but problems remain

      CBT (cognitive behavioural therapy) is a talking therapy that teaches practical skills. For young people with ADHD it covers getting on with friends, solving problems, self-control, listening, and handling and expressing feelings.

    3
    Adults

    Instead of medicine, or alongside it

    For adults, non-drug treatment is an option in its own right: instead of medicine, or added to it.

    1. 1
      Medicine when changes are not enough

      If ADHD still causes significant problems in at least one area of life after practical changes (for example at work), offer medicine.

    2. 2
      Non-drug treatment instead of medicine

      Consider it for adults who have weighed it up and choose not to take medicine, who find it hard to take medicine regularly, or for whom medicine did not work or caused side effects they could not tolerate.

    3. 3
      Non-drug treatment alongside medicine

      Consider it when medicine helps but significant problems remain, for example with planning, time or emotions.

    4. 4
      What the minimum offer looks like

      At least a structured supportive psychological intervention focused on ADHD: planned sessions that work on the problems ADHD causes in the person's life, plus regular follow-up in person or by phone. It may include parts of CBT, or a full course.

      In practiceA leaflet or a single session does not meet this.

    Diet, supplements and lifestyle

    Support healthy habits. Avoid blanket restrictions. Investigate a suspected food trigger carefully.

    1
    For children, young people and adults

    Build healthy everyday habits

    NICE asks clinicians to stress the value of a balanced diet, good nutrition and regular exercise for children, young people and adults with ADHD.

    Why it matters: these help general health, mood and sleep. Regular meals matter even more when ADHD medicine reduces appetite. They support ADHD care; on their own they do not treat ADHD.

    Balanced diet

    Variety, rather than a special “ADHD diet”.

    • Fruit and vegetables
    • Wholegrains and protein foods
    • Water through the day

    Adequate nutrition

    Make eating practical and predictable.

    • Plan regular meals
    • Keep easy options available
    • Discuss appetite concerns

    Regular movement

    Choose activity the person enjoys.

    • Walk, play, cycle or dance
    • Start with manageable steps
    • Build it into daily life

    Supportive routines

    Practical ideas to discuss (extra to the NICE advice).

    • A consistent wind-down
    • Simple meal reminders
    • Plans that fit family life

    The distinction: healthy lifestyle support complements ADHD care; it does not replace it.

    2
    Children and young people · ADHD treatment

    Do not offer these as ADHD treatment

    Families often ask about two diet ideas they have read about online or heard from friends: cutting out food colourings, and taking omega-3 (fish oil) supplements. NICE looked at the evidence for both and says do not use them to treat ADHD in children and young people.

    Not a general treatment

    Removing colourings & additives

    What families hear

    Artificial colourings (the E-numbers in sweets and fizzy drinks) and additives cause ADHD, so a diet without them will treat it.

    What NICE says

    Do not advise cutting them out as a general treatment for ADHD.

    Why

    A large UK trial in children from the general population found that certain colouring mixes slightly increased hyperactive behaviour, and a later review estimated that only a minority of children with ADHD (around 1 in 12) may react to colourings. The effect was small, so removing them is not a treatment for ADHD in general. A blanket ban also makes shopping, school meals and parties harder.

    What to tell families

    There is no need to ban these foods. A balanced diet matters more than avoiding every additive.

    A specific concern is different.If a family notices that one particular food or drink seems to change behaviour, check it with the diary pathway below.
    Do not offer for ADHD

    Omega-3 & fatty-acid supplements

    What families hear

    Fish oil (omega-3) capsules "feed the brain" and improve attention.

    What NICE says

    Do not advise or offer omega-3 or other fatty acid supplements to treat ADHD.

    Why

    Reviews of the trials disagree. Some found a small benefit on ADHD symptoms; the Cochrane review found no clear benefit over placebo. At best the effect is small, much smaller than with ADHD medicine. Families may spend money on supplements, or delay a treatment that works, for little gain.

    What to tell families

    Supplements are not an ADHD treatment. If they want to take them for other reasons, that is their choice, but it should not replace the treatment plan.

    Food is not the same as a supplement.Oily fish, nuts and seeds are good foods in a normal diet. This advice is only about capsules and supplements taken as an ADHD treatment.

    A suspected trigger · children and young people

    Check the link before changing the diet

    A structured conversation, not an automatic food ban.

    Why this order: the diary shows whether the link is real before anyone cuts foods out, and the dietitian makes sure the child still gets the nutrition they need to grow.

    1. 1

      Ask

      Does a food or drink seem to affect hyperactive behaviour?

    2. 2

      Keep a diary

      If a clear link is reported, the family records food, drink and behaviour.

    3. 3

      Look for a pattern

      Review the diary together. Does it support a link?

    4. 4

      Refer to a dietitian

      Only if the diary supports a link. Discuss the diary and nutritional needs.

      Dietitian, specialist and family jointly manage any specific elimination, and review it.

    Know the uncertainty

    “Few foods” diets

    A "few foods" diet cuts a child's diet down to a small number of foods, then adds foods back one at a time to see which cause problems.

    NICE says to tell families: there is only limited evidence of short-term benefit, and no evidence about long-term effectiveness or possible harms.

    A highly restrictive diet is not a routine next step.

    Separate evidence from assumptions

    What about sugar?

    Controlled studies have not found a consistent overall effect on children’s behaviour or cognition. This does not prove that every child responds identically.

    Medication: children 5 and over

    For children aged 5 and over and young people, medication is offered when symptoms still cause persistent, significant impairment in at least one area of life after environmental changes, once the options have been discussed and the baseline is done. NICE then sets a clear order.

    1
    Methylphenidate

    Short- or long-acting. Titrate to the best tolerated dose.

    2

    2b

    Medication: adults

    For adults, NICE offers medication when symptoms still cause significant impairment in at least one area after environmental changes. The first choice is shared.

    1
    Lisdexamfetamine or methylphenidate

    Decide together, using the person’s preferences, coexisting conditions, misuse risk and practical needs.

    2
    Switch to the other

    If a 6-week trial at an adequate dose has not given enough benefit.

    2b
    Dexamfetamine

    If the person responds to lisdexamfetamine but cannot tolerate how long it lasts. Off-label in adults.

    3
    Atomoxetine

    If either stimulant is not tolerated, or neither worked after separate 6-week trials.

    !
    No response to stimulants and a non-stimulant

    Get a second opinion or refer to a tertiary service. Guanfacine in adults, clonidine and other medicines only with tertiary advice.

    Non-drug treatment is not a hurdle. Adults do not need to try CBT before medication. It is offered instead of medication if they prefer, or alongside it if impairment remains.
    Why lisdexamfetamine or methylphenidate first in adults?

    NICE allows either first in adults. A large network meta-analysis found amfetamines slightly more effective in adults and methylphenidate the best balance of benefit and tolerability in children (Cortese 2018). In practice, local formulary, supply, misuse risk and the person’s preference guide the choice.

    The 6-week rule and the pathway walker

    Before saying a medicine “has not worked”, check it had a fair trial: 6 weeks at an adequate dose. Adequate means titrated to the best tolerated dose, not a few weeks at the starting dose. The methylphenidate SmPCs add: stop if there is no improvement after suitable dose adjustment over about a month.

    School and work

    Most of a person's day is spent at school, college or work, so that is where ADHD shows most. Getting the right support there is part of treatment. Work through the three tabs.

    1
    School, college and university

    Talk to the school, with consent

    NICE asks the ADHD team to contact the school, college or university, after getting consent.

    1. 1
      When to get in touch

      At diagnosis, whenever symptoms change, and at every move: to a new school, from school to college, and from college to university.

    2. 2
      Ask for consent first

      Agree with the young person (and parent or carer, if appropriate) what will be shared and with whom.

    3. 3
      What to explain
      • The diagnosis: ADHD is a real diagnosis, and how it is likely to affect school life.
      • Other conditions: anything else, such as a learning disability, is separate and may need different help.
      • The plan: the treatment plan and any special educational needs, with ideas for adjustments in class.
      • Feedback: what the school notices helps the young person and the ADHD team.
    2
    Work

    Rights and support at work

    Adults with ADHD may have a legal right to changes at work, and can get practical help to pay for support.

    1. 1
      Does ADHD count as a disability?

      Under the Equality Act 2010, a condition is a disability if it has a substantial, long-term effect on normal daily activities. Many adults with ADHD meet this, but it depends on the person.

    2. 2
      Reasonable adjustments

      If ADHD counts as a disability and the employer knows, or could reasonably be expected to know, the employer must make reasonable changes so the person is not at a disadvantage.

      In practiceTelling an employer is the person's choice. With consent, a letter to the employer or occupational health can explain ADHD and suggest adjustments.

    3. 3
      Access to Work

      A government grant (England, Scotland and Wales; Northern Ireland has its own scheme) for practical help beyond what the employer must provide: for example equipment, software, a job coach, or a mental health support service. It does not pay for the adjustments the employer is legally required to make.

    3
    Adjustments

    Changes that often help

    Small, practical changes to the environment. Ask the person what helps them most; there is no single list.

    At school or college
    • A seat near the teacher and away from distractions.
    • Instructions written down and broken into small steps.
    • Short movement breaks.
    • A homework planner and regular check-ins.
    • Extra time or rest breaks in exams, if the school applies for them.
    At work
    • A quieter workspace, or noise-cancelling headphones.
    • Written instructions, agendas and deadlines.
    • Regular, short check-ins with a manager.
    • Flexible start times and planned breaks.
    • Planning tools, reminders and help to prioritise tasks.

    These are the "environmental modifications" NICE expects to be in place, and reviewed, before deciding whether medicine is needed.

    Methylphenidate: doses by product

    Methylphenidate is one medicine sold as many different products: immediate-release tablets, and several modified-release tablets and capsules made by different companies, each with its own licence. The active drug is the same, but the products are not interchangeable, because three things change from product to product:

    1. 1
      How it releases the drug

      This decides when it starts working and how long it lasts: about 3–4 hours for immediate release, about 8 or 12 hours for modified release. The same dose in a different product can give a different day.

    2. 2
      Who it is licensed for

      Six products are licensed for children only (for example Equasym XL, Matoride XL and Medikinet tablets; the full list is under the picker), so using them in adults is off-label.

    3. 3
      The doses and how to take it

      The starting dose, step size and maximum differ, and so do the rules on food and swallowing. For example, Medikinet XL must be taken with food, and Concerta XL must be swallowed whole.

    So: always prescribe, dispense and check by brand, and look up that brand's SmPC. The MHRA advises care when switching between modified-release products, because a different release pattern can change how well symptoms are controlled.

    3. Medikinet XL (50:50). The larger immediate portion gives a stronger morning effect. A second rise about 3 hours later keeps levels at or above 75% of the peak for 3–4 hours, and it lasts about 8 hours. It is taken with or after breakfast (SPC), so check that breakfast is actually eaten.

    Which products can be swapped?

    Two products are bioequivalent when the same dose gives the same blood levels over time. Only then is a like-for-like swap safe (SPS, updated 28 Aug 2025).

    Fitting the dose to the day

    A good regimen covers the hours that matter, such as school, homework, the drive to work or evening study, and wears off before bed. Often the fix is a different shape, not a bigger dose.

    What NICE says

    Why once-daily MR

    Convenience, better adherence, less stigma (no dose at school or work), no controlled drug to store at school, less misuse and diversion.

    When IR helps

    During first titration to find the dose, or when a flexible regimen is needed.

    Extending the day

    MR methylphenidate in the morning plus IR methylphenidate later in the day. Effect, duration and side effects vary from person to person.

    In clinic

    What the patient notices

    Lisdexamfetamine builds smoothly over the morning and lasts into the evening. Dexamfetamine works within the hour and wears off after a few hours (clinical practice).

    Side effects to expect

    Like methylphenidate: less appetite, trouble getting to sleep, a faster pulse and higher blood pressure. A long effect can push sleep problems later.

    Practical point

    Lisdexamfetamine’s slow conversion lowers, but does not remove, misuse potential. Urine pH changes how fast amfetamines leave the body (Module 7).

    Before you prescribe: contraindications

    A contraindication means “do not use”. A caution or warning (4.4) means “use, but with extra care and monitoring”. Check both against the baseline history from Module 3 before the first dose.

    Methylphenidate

    Concerta XL SmPC 4.3; other brands have similar lists, so check each one.

    Do not use (4.3)
    • Glaucoma; phaeochromocytoma; hyperthyroidism or thyrotoxicosis
    • Non-selective, irreversible MAOIs now or in the last 14 days
    • Severe depression, anorexia nervosa, suicidal tendencies, psychotic symptoms, severe mood disorders, mania, schizophrenia, psychopathic or borderline personality disorder; poorly controlled type I bipolar disorder
    • Pre-existing cardiovascular disease (for example severe hypertension, heart failure, angina, MI, cardiomyopathy, life-threatening arrhythmias, channelopathies) or cerebrovascular disease (aneurysm, vasculitis, stroke)
    Use with care (4.4)
    • Blood pressure and pulse rises
    • New or worse psychosis, mania, aggression, anxiety
    • Tics, seizures, growth, misuse and diversion
    • Priapism, mainly around dose changes: urgent help

    “Has anyone in the family died suddenly of a heart problem under 40? Do you faint or get breathless with exercise?”

    Many psychiatric contraindications are about current, severe or uncontrolled illness. Stabilise first with the right specialist, then reconsider.

    Switching, stopping and controlled drug rules

    Three stimulants are Schedule 2 controlled drugs under the Misuse of Drugs Regulations 2001. NICE expects prescribers to know these rules. First, how to change or stop treatment safely.

    Changing treatment

    Switching

    Neither NICE nor the SmPCs set a washout between methylphenidate and lisdexamfetamine; because both are short-lived, many services start the new one the next morning (clinical practice). Within methylphenidate, switch only within a bioequivalent group (lesson 3). Leave the MAOI gaps (lesson 7).

    Stopping

    Guanfacine: taper, no more than 1 mg every 3–7 days. Dexamfetamine: taper; sudden stopping can cause extreme tiredness and low mood. Atomoxetine: no withdrawal syndrome; taper unless side effects are serious.

    Planned breaks

    NICE: consider trial periods off or on a lower dose when the balance suggests it, and a school-holiday break if height is affected. Review at least yearly.

    2. Prescribe MR methylphenidate by brand. Swap only within a bioequivalent group; Equasym XL never.
    3. Know the ceilings: methylphenidate 60 mg child, 72–80 mg adult; lisdexamfetamine 70 mg (50 mg in severe renal impairment); atomoxetine 100 mg; guanfacine 4–7 mg by weight.
    4. Titrate to optimisation, not to the maximum, in weekly steps with scales, pulse and BP; slower with coexisting conditions; judge after 6 weeks at an adequate dose.
    5. Schedule 2 rules: words and figures, 28-day validity, up to 30 days’ supply, no emergency supply. Taper guanfacine; never stop it suddenly.

    Starting an ADHD medicine is the easy part. Keeping it safe and worthwhile for years is the real work: regular checks of growth, heart rate and blood pressure, sleep, mood and misuse, a yearly review of whether it is still needed, and clear teamwork between the specialist and primary care. This module teaches you to run all of it.

    By the end of this module you can
    • State what NICE asks you to monitor, how often, for children and adults
    • Measure and interpret heart rate, blood pressure and growth, and act on the NICE thresholds
    • Recognise sleep, mood, tic, seizure and sexual adverse effects and the NICE action for each
    • Judge whether treatment is working, and spot adherence problems and diversion
    • Run a complete annual review and plan a safe trial without medication
    • Work within a shared care protocol: who does what, what the GP needs, and when to hand back
    • Document monitoring and decisions so another clinician can act on them

    Why monitor, and the rhythm of care

    Monitoring means checking, at set times, that a medicine is still helping and is not causing harm. ADHD medicines are usually taken for years, often by growing children, and most raise heart rate a little and reduce appetite. Small changes add up, so NICE sets a regular rhythm of checks.

    Heart rate and blood pressure

    Stimulants and atomoxetine raise heart rate and blood pressure a little in most people, and a lot in a few. Guanfacine does the opposite: it can lower both. The skill is to measure well, compare with the right normal range, and act only on confirmed results, but act promptly when they are.

    1
    Measure properly

    Seated and rested, with an appropriately sized cuff (a cuff that is too small reads falsely high). Note the time of the last dose.

    2
    Compare with the normal range for age

    Adults: usual adult ranges. Children and young people: blood pressure centiles for age, sex and height, because “normal” changes as they grow.

    3
    One high reading? Repeat it

    Re-measure at rest. Look for raisers: caffeine and energy drinks, decongestants (pseudoephedrine), anxiety, recent exercise, a missed antihypertensive.

    Is it sustained: confirmed on 2 occasions?
    Not sustainedDocument and recheck

    Record it, compare with earlier readings, and recheck at the next scheduled check or sooner if rising.

    SustainedReduce the dose and refer

    Resting heart rate over 120 bpm, an arrhythmia, or systolic BP above the 95th centile (or a clinically significant rise): reduce the dose and refer to a paediatric hypertension specialist or an adult physician.

    !
    Guanfacine: low, not high

    Sustained orthostatic hypotension (BP falls on standing, causing dizziness) or fainting: reduce the dose or switch to another ADHD medicine. Never stop guanfacine suddenly: BP can rebound.

    Growth, weight and appetite

    Stimulants reduce appetite, most at midday when the medicine is working hardest. In children this can slow weight gain and, in some, height gain. A single low number means little: what matters is the trend on a growth chart.

    In clinic

    Measure

    Height with a stadiometer (a fixed height measure), shoes off, head level. Weight on the same scales, in light clothes.

    Plot

    Children and young people: plot both on a growth chart (in the UK, the UK-WHO charts from the RCPCH) and make sure the treating clinician reviews them.

    Read the trend

    Compare with the child’s own centile line before treatment. Falling across centile lines matters more than one low reading.

    If weight loss is a concern: the six NICE options

    • Take it with or after foodnot before meals
    • Eat when the effect wears offextra meals or snacks early morning or late evening
    • Dietary advicefrom a dietitian or the team
    • High-calorie, nutritious foodsenergy-dense food of good nutritional value
    • A planned breakfrom treatment, agreed with the specialist
    • Change the medicineif weight change persists
    !
    Height falling behind

    If a child or young person has not met the height expected for their age because of medication, consider a planned break over school holidays to allow catch-up growth. This is a specialist decision, shared with the family.

    Adults. Weigh every 6 months. If weight has changed because of treatment, consider monitoring BMI, and change the medication if the change persists. Guanfacine can increase BMI, so check it more often in the first year.

    Sleep, mood, tics and seizures

    These are the adverse effects you find only by asking. Each tab shows what people say, what else it could be, a good question, and the NICE action. Many of these problems were there before treatment, which is why the baseline matters.

    Sleep

    Trouble falling asleep is common on stimulants, but many people with ADHD slept badly before treatment too. Compare with the baseline sleep history.

    What people say

    “He’s wide awake at 11 at night.”

    “I can’t switch my brain off since the dose went up.”

    Could it be something else?
    • ADHD itself: late sleep onset is common without medication
    • Wearing off: rebound restlessness in the evening
    • Other causes: screens, caffeine, anxiety, low mood, snoring or sleep apnoea
    Ask this

    “What time is the last dose taken, and what time do you fall asleep? Was it like this before the medicine?”

    What to do

    Monitor changes in sleep, for example with a sleep diary, and adjust the medication: earlier dose, lower afternoon dose, shorter-acting product, or a switch. Sleep hygiene first; melatonin usually only on specialist advice (local protocols).

    Mood and behaviour

    Medicines can cause irritability, tearfulness, anxiety or a “flat”, over-focused state, usually dose-related. Rarely they trigger psychosis or mania. Atomoxetine carries a warning for suicidal thoughts.

    What people say

    “She’s like a zombie at school.”

    “He’s much angrier in the evenings.”

    Could it be something else?
    • Dose too high: flat, withdrawn, tearful
    • Rebound: irritable as the dose wears off
    • A coexisting condition: depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, or a different diagnosis altogether
    Ask this

    “Since starting, have you felt low, flat, more anxious or more irritable? Any thoughts of harming yourself?”

    What to do

    If behaviour worsens, adjust the medication and review the diagnosis. Acute psychosis or mania: stop ADHD medication; consider restarting after it has resolved. Suicidal thoughts: urgent assessment.

    Tics

    Tics are sudden repeated movements or sounds. They often occur with ADHD and naturally wax and wane, so a new tic on a stimulant is not always caused by it.

    What people say

    “He keeps blinking and sniffing.”

    “The throat-clearing started in spring.”

    Could it be something else?
    • Natural course: tics come and go, worse with stress and tiredness
    • Coincidence: tics often begin in the primary-school years whether or not a stimulant is taken
    Ask this

    “When did the tics start, have they changed with the dose, and are they bothering you or getting in the way?”

    What to do

    Decide whether the tics are stimulant-related and whether their impairment outweighs the benefit of treatment. If related: reduce the stimulant dose, or consider guanfacine (ages 5–17), atomoxetine or clonidine (under 18s only after tertiary advice), or stopping.

    Seizures

    ADHD is more common in people with epilepsy. Stimulant SmPCs advise caution, because seizure threshold may be lowered.

    What people say

    “She had a fit for the first time last week.”

    “My seizures have been more frequent.”

    Could it be something else?
    • Epilepsy itself: changes in seizure pattern happen for many reasons
    • Missed antiepileptic doses, poor sleep, alcohol
    Ask this

    “Any new fits, blank spells or changes in your usual seizure pattern?”

    What to do

    Review the ADHD medication and stop any that might be contributing. After investigation, cautiously reintroduce it if it is unlikely to be the cause.

    Sexual function

    Atomoxetine can cause erectile and ejaculatory problems. People rarely mention it unless asked.

    What people say

    “I didn’t think it was related to the tablets.”

    Could it be something else?
    • Other medicines: SSRIs, antihypertensives
    • Mood, alcohol, relationship stress, physical illness
    Ask this

    Ask privately and plainly: “Some people on this medicine notice changes in erections or ejaculation. Has that happened to you?”

    What to do

    Monitor young people and adults on atomoxetine for sexual dysfunction. Discuss a dose change or switch with the specialist. Priapism (a painful erection lasting over 2 hours), reported with methylphenidate and atomoxetine, is an emergency.

    Is it working? Benefit, adherence and misuse

    Safety checks are only half of monitoring. The other half is benefit: is this medicine still making a real difference to the person’s life, at an acceptable cost?

    Measure benefit

    Same scale, same raters

    Use standard symptom and side-effect rating scales through treatment, ideally the ones used at baseline, so scores can be compared.

    Function, not just symptoms

    Check the goals set at the start: school reports, work, driving, relationships, money. Ask how well it works through the whole day.

    Side-effect checklist

    Ask people to keep their own record of side effects between visits. It turns vague memories into evidence.

    Adherence: ADHD makes it harder

    Remembering to order, collect and take a medicine is exactly what ADHD makes difficult. Missed doses are a symptom to solve, not a sign the person “doesn’t care”.

    • Correct misconceptionsfor example, medication does not change personality
    • Clear written or picture instructionskept with the medicine, such as a sticker on the pack
    • Visual remindersapps, alarms, pill organisers, notes on the fridge
    • Build it into a routinewith breakfast or after brushing teeth; parents oversee children’s doses

    Misuse and diversion

    Diversion means the medicine going to someone it was not prescribed for, for example to friends to study or to lose weight. Risk changes with age and circumstances (starting university, leaving home), so keep watching.

    Warning signs
    • Early or lost prescriptionsRepeated early requests, “lost” or “stolen” supplies (clinical practice).
    • Asking for immediate-releaseOr for higher doses without a clear reason (clinical practice).
    • Substance misuseIn the person or household, or peers who ask for stimulants.
    What NICE says to do
    • Be cautiousabout stimulants if there is a risk of diversion for study or appetite suppression.
    • Choose lower-risk productsDo not offer immediate-release stimulants or modified-release ones that can easily be injected or snorted where there is risk.
    • Keep monitoringClinicians and parents watch for changes in risk.
    Keep the brand the same. Long-acting methylphenidate products release the medicine in different patterns, so prescribe by brand and check the brand has not changed when someone reports a sudden change in effect.

    The annual review

    At least once a year, a healthcare professional with training and expertise in ADHD reviews the medication and decides, with the person, whether it should continue. This can be an independent prescribing pharmacist working within their competence in an ADHD service.

    The eight areas NICE asks you to cover

    • Preferenceof the person, and family or carers
    • Benefitsincluding how well it works through the day
    • Adverse effectsfrom the monitoring record and by asking
    • Clinical needand whether the dose is optimised
    • Education and workimpact on study and employment
    • Missed doses and breakswhat happens on days without it
    • Other conditionseffect on mental, physical and neurodevelopmental conditions
    • Support needspsychological, educational or social help if impairment remains
    NICE and the SmPCs say it differently. NICE: consider trial periods off medication or dose reductions when the balance of benefits and harms suggests it, and document the reasons if you continue. The methylphenidate SmPC goes further: treatment “should not and need not be indefinite”, and it recommends stopping at least once a year to check the person’s condition. The Intuniv SmPC asks you to re-evaluate guanfacine every 3 months in the first year, then at least yearly. Know both, then decide with the person.

    In clinic

    Let them lead

    Encourage people to say if they want to stop or change, and involve them in any decision about stopping.

    Write down why

    If the decision is to continue, record the reasons. “Continue, no change” is not a reason.

    Plan transition

    Young people should be reassessed at school-leaving age, with a smooth move to adult services usually completed by 18.

    Use the planner to work through each area and record the decision. You can download the plan as text.

    Treatment breaks and trials without medication

    A trial without medication (sometimes called a drug holiday or treatment break) is a planned period off the medicine to see whether it is still needed, or to let a child catch up on growth. Planned well, it answers a real question. Unplanned, it just causes a bad month.

    1
    Agree the question

    “Is it still needed?”, or “can growth catch up?”. Decide together, and agree what “worse” would look like.

    2
    Choose the time

    A settled period, not exams, a new job or a house move. For growth, school holidays. Trial discontinuations are managed by the specialist under shared care.

    3
    Record a baseline on treatment

    The same rating scales from home and school or work, completed before stopping.

    4
    Stop safely

    Stimulants: stop as planned, with supervision, because stopping may unmask low mood and over-activity. Atomoxetine: may be tapered. Guanfacine: taper by no more than 1 mg every 3–7 days.

    After the trial period, what do the ratings show?
    Stays wellStay off, keep follow-up

    Keep reviewing according to severity, even without medication.

    Symptoms and impairment returnRestart

    Restart and document the evidence that it is still needed, the reason to continue.

    Shared care: who does what

    Shared care means the specialist and primary care (usually the GP practice, sometimes a practice pharmacist prescriber) share responsibility for one person’s medicine under a written shared care protocol. NICE says that after titration and dose stabilisation, prescribing and monitoring should be done under shared care arrangements with primary care.

    1
    Specialist starts and titrates

    Assessment, baseline, consent, titration to a stable dose, with the specialist prescribing.

    2
    Specialist asks primary care to share care

    A letter or form with everything the GP needs (below). The specialist prescribes enough to cover any delay.

    3
    Primary care replies

    Agrees, asks questions or declines, ideally within 14 days. Shared care is voluntary: a prescriber should not take on prescribing they are not confident to manage.

    4
    Care transfers

    Typically after at least 12 weeks, once the dose is optimised and results have been satisfactory for at least 4 weeks (national atomoxetine protocol; local protocols vary).

    !
    The specialist does not disappear

    The specialist keeps the annual review, manages trial discontinuations, gives advice and takes people back quickly when needed.

    Who does what

    Specialist

    Diagnoses; does the baseline; discusses options and gets consent; explains any off-label use; titrates; sends the request; reviews at least yearly and tells primary care the outcome in writing; advises on side effects, pregnancy and stopping.

    Primary care

    Prescribes the agreed product and dose; does the monitoring in the protocol; checks interactions when new medicines start; manages side effects as the protocol says; refers back when needed.

    Patient and carer

    Takes it as prescribed; attends monitoring and reviews (treatment may stop if they do not); reports side effects; tells other prescribers and pharmacists; keeps contact details up to date.

    What the GP needs in the request

    • Diagnosisand why medication was chosen
    • Exact productbrand, strength, dose and timing
    • Resultsbaseline and latest height, weight, pulse and BP
    • What is due whennext monitoring dates and annual review date
    • What to do if…action for abnormal results
    • Contactsnamed clinician and a fast route back to the team

    When to hand back to the specialist

    • Thresholds crossedsustained HR over 120, arrhythmia, significant BP rise
    • Mental health changenew or worse mood, psychosis, mania or suicidal thoughts
    • Seizuresnew or more frequent: stop contributing medicine and refer urgently
    • Pregnancyplanned or confirmed
    • Not attendingnon-adherence to monitoring or treatment
    • Possibly no longer neededwell for at least a year, or no symptoms on missed days: ask about a trial off (national SCP)

    Most side effects of ADHD medicines are expected, depend on the dose and the time of day, and can be fixed. A few are rare but serious and need action the same day. This module teaches you to tell them apart, to spot the interactions that matter, and to give safe advice in pregnancy, epilepsy, surgery, older age, driving and travel.

    By the end of this module you can
    • Triage a reported side effect: manage it, review it soon, or act the same day
    • Manage appetite, weight, sleep, mood and tic problems using NICE NG87 1.8
    • Recognise the drug-specific warnings for methylphenidate, amfetamines, atomoxetine and guanfacine
    • Assess cardiovascular risk before and during treatment, and know when to involve cardiology
    • Check the interactions that matter, including MAOIs, CYP2D6 inhibitors with atomoxetine and CYP3A4 drugs with guanfacine
    • Advise on alcohol, street drugs, misuse and diversion
    • Give accurate advice on pregnancy, breastfeeding, epilepsy, surgery, older age, driving and travel

    Side effects: how to think

    An adverse effect (side effect) is an unwanted effect of a medicine taken at a normal dose. With ADHD medicines, most are predictable. They follow the dose and the clock, so the fix is usually a change of dose, timing or product, not stopping treatment.

    Three kinds of side effect

    Peak effects

    Strongest while the medicine is working: poor appetite at lunch, headache, feeling “wired”, a faster pulse. They grow with dose increases. Fix: dose, timing, food.

    Wear-off effects

    Appear as the level falls, usually late afternoon: irritability, tearfulness, symptoms rushing back (“rebound”). Fix: smoother profile or a planned later dose.

    Rare and serious

    Not simply dose-related: liver injury, psychosis or mania, seizures, priapism, fainting. Fix: stop or act the same day, then a specialist decision.

    Four questions before you blame the drug

    1
    When does it happen?

    Match it to dose times. Peak, wear-off or all day? A pattern tied to the clock points to the medicine.

    2
    What changed?

    A dose increase, a different brand of modified-release methylphenidate, a new medicine, an over-the-counter (OTC) decongestant, caffeine, alcohol, or a life event.

    3
    Was it there before treatment?

    Poor sleep, irritability and low appetite are common in untreated ADHD. Compare with the baseline record.

    4
    How serious is it?

    Decide which lane it is in (below), and record it in the notes.

    Which lane?
    ManageableAdvise, adjust and monitor

    Appetite, mild sleep delay, headache, nausea, mild anxiety. Use the fixes in lessons 2–4 and review.

    Persistent or impairingPrescriber review within days

    Weight loss, tics that bother the person, mood change, raised pulse or BP on one reading.

    !
    Red flags: act the same day

    Hallucinations or mania, suicidal thoughts, chest pain or fainting, a seizure, an erection lasting hours, jaundice or dark urine on atomoxetine.

    Help people track it. NICE asks prescribers to record effectiveness and adverse effects, and to encourage people to keep their own adverse-effect checklist. Report suspected adverse reactions on the MHRA Yellow Card scheme.
    How to explain it to a patient

    “Most side effects show up at the same time each day and can be fixed by changing the dose, the time you take it or the product. A few need a call the same day, and I’ll tell you exactly which ones.”

    Appetite, weight and sleep

    These are the most common reasons people struggle with stimulants. Both follow the clock: appetite is lowest while the medicine works, and sleep suffers if it is still working at bedtime.

    In clinic

    Poor appetite

    Stimulants and atomoxetine. Worst at lunchtime, back by evening. Watch for skipped meals, weight loss and, in children, slower growth.

    Sleep delay

    Mainly stimulants still active at bedtime (late or long-acting doses). Remember that ADHD itself often causes sleep problems.

    Weight gain

    The opposite problem with guanfacine: children and young people may show an increase in body mass index (BMI).

    If weight loss is a concern: the six NICE options

    • Take with or after foodnot before meals: a good breakfast first
    • Eat when it has worn offextra meals or snacks early morning or late evening
    • Dietary advicefrom the team or a dietitian
    • High-calorie, nutritious foodsnot just snacks and sugary drinks
    • A planned treatment breakfor example over school holidays, agreed with the specialist
    • Change the medicationif weight loss persists despite the above

    Adults: consider monitoring body mass index (BMI) if weight changes because of treatment, and change the medicine if the change persists. Children: if height falls behind what is expected, consider a planned break over school holidays for “catch-up” growth.

    Sleep: what to try

    • Keep a sleep diarybedtime, time asleep, dose times; adjust medication accordingly
    • Move doses earlierand avoid late top-up doses
    • Shorten the profilea product that wears off earlier (see the day-profile tool in Module 5)
    • Sleep hygiene and caffeineroutine, screens, energy drinks and coffee

    Melatonin for sleep-onset problems in children with ADHD is a specialist or shared-care decision under local protocols (Clinical practice).

    Drug-specific warnings

    Each ADHD medicine has its own “watch-outs” in the summary of product characteristics (SmPC, the official prescribing document). Choose a medicine.

    Methylphenidate

    Blocks dopamine and noradrenaline reuptake. Its side effects are those of a stimulant: they peak with the dose and settle as it wears off.

    Common and usually manageable
    • Poor appetite, weight loss
    • Trouble getting to sleep
    • Headache, tummy ache, nervousness
    • Raised pulse and BP
    • Cold fingers and toes; Raynaud’s phenomenon
    Serious warnings
    • Priapism: prolonged, painful erections reported
    • New or worse seizures: stop
    • New tics, psychosis, mania, aggression, suicidal thoughts
    • Cerebral vasculitis: very rare
    Ask this

    “Has anything changed since the last supply: a different brand, a new medicine, or a cold remedy?”

    What to do

    Manage peak and wear-off effects by dose, timing or product. Any erection lasting hours: emergency care now. Do not switch between modified-release brands without care: release profiles differ.

    Lisdexamfetamine and dexamfetamine

    Lisdexamfetamine is a prodrug: the body turns it into dexamfetamine. Both release dopamine and noradrenaline, so the stimulant side effects are the same as methylphenidate’s.

    Common and usually manageable
    • Poor appetite, weight loss, dry mouth
    • Insomnia, headache, irritability
    • Teeth grinding (bruxism): common in adults
    • Erectile dysfunction: common in adults on lisdexamfetamine
    Serious warnings
    • QTc prolongation seen in some patients on lisdexamfetamine: caution with long QT or QT-prolonging drugs
    • Psychosis, mania, aggression, tics
    • New or worse seizures: stop
    • Potential for misuse, dependence and diversion
    Ask this

    “Any chest pain on exertion, fainting, or a racing heart?” Exertional chest pain or unexplained fainting needs a prompt cardiac evaluation.

    What to do

    Same approach as methylphenidate. Dexamfetamine (Amfexa) has extra contraindications: history of drug or alcohol abuse, Tourette syndrome, porphyria. Amfetamines can raise plasma corticosteroid levels, which affects some lab tests.

    Atomoxetine

    Blocks noradrenaline reuptake. It works gradually and all day. It is broken down by the liver enzyme CYP2D6, which matters for interactions (lesson 6).

    Common and usually manageable
    • Nausea, tummy ache, poor appetite: take with food or split the dose
    • Tiredness or sleep problems
    • Raised pulse and BP: 8–12% of children and 6–10% of adults had more pronounced rises
    • Erectile and ejaculatory dysfunction: ask young people and adults
    Serious warnings
    • Liver injury (very rare): jaundice or lab evidence means stop and never restart
    • Suicidal thoughts: more frequent in children and adolescents in trials
    • Hostility and emotional lability; psychosis or mania
    • Seizures: consider stopping; caution with long QT
    • Priapism: rare
    Ask this

    “Any yellow skin or eyes, dark urine, itching or pain under the right ribs? Any thoughts of harming yourself?”

    What to do

    Counsel on liver and mood symptoms before starting. No routine liver tests are needed: test if symptoms appear. Jaundice or liver injury: stop for good. Suicidal thoughts: same-day review.

    Guanfacine

    An alpha-2A agonist: it lowers sympathetic “fight or flight” tone. So BP and pulse go down, and sleepiness is common. Licensed for ages 6–17 only.

    Common and usually manageable
    • Sleepiness and sedation, mainly at the start
    • Low BP, slow pulse, dizziness on standing
    • Weight gain and higher BMI
    • Tiredness, headache, tummy ache
    Serious warnings
    • Fainting (syncope), marked low BP or bradycardia
    • Rebound high BP if stopped suddenly; hypertensive encephalopathy very rarely reported
    • QT prolongation: caution with a history of long QT
    • Suicide-related events (post-marketing reports)
    Ask this

    “Do you feel dizzy when you stand up, or have you fainted? Have any doses been missed?”

    What to do

    Sustained orthostatic hypotension or fainting: reduce the dose or switch. Never stop suddenly: taper by no more than 1 mg every 3–7 days, monitoring BP and pulse. If 2 or more doses in a row are missed, re-titrate. No alcohol.

    Street drugs

    Cannabis

    Common “self-medication”. It can worsen motivation, memory, sleep and anxiety, raises the pulse, and increases psychosis risk. It also muddies the assessment of how well treatment works.

    Cocaine and street amfetamines

    Add to the stimulant effect: high pulse and BP, arrhythmia, chest pain, stroke risk. A strong reason to choose a lower-misuse product and agree a safety plan.

    MDMA (ecstasy)

    Sympathomimetic and serotonergic: adds both a cardiovascular and a serotonin syndrome risk, as with serotonergic medicines (lesson 6).

    Street-drug effects in this card set: Clinical practice (no SmPC interaction data). Use harm-reduction advice and involve drug and alcohol services.

    Prescribing when misuse or diversion is a risk

    Diversion means medicine going to someone it was not prescribed for (sold, shared or stolen), for example to stay awake for exams or to lose weight.

    1
    Assess risk at baseline

    Substance misuse and diversion risk are part of the pre-treatment assessment.

    2
    Choose a lower-risk product

    Be cautious with stimulants if diversion for “cognitive enhancement” or appetite suppression is likely. Atomoxetine is not a controlled drug. Lisdexamfetamine is a prodrug, but its SmPC still warns of misuse potential.

    3
    Titrate slowly, monitor more

    Substance misuse is on the NICE list for slower titration and more frequent monitoring.

    4
    Keep watching

    Risk changes with age and circumstances: a new university flat, a new partner, money problems. Watch for early requests and “lost” prescriptions.

    !
    If there is a risk of misuse or diversion

    Do not offer immediate-release stimulants, or modified-release stimulants that can easily be injected or snorted. Dexamfetamine (Amfexa) is contraindicated with a history of drug or alcohol abuse.

    Pregnancy and breastfeeding

    The safety data are limited and mostly observational (they show links, not proof of cause). NICE NG87 does not cover pregnancy, so advice comes from the SmPCs and the UK Teratology Information Service (UKTIS). The key is a planned, shared decision: the risks of the medicine weighed against the risks of untreated ADHD, such as accidents, driving and coping with a new baby.

    1
    Before pregnancy

    Discuss contraception and pregnancy plans with everyone who could become pregnant. Guanfacine is not recommended without contraception. The dexamfetamine SmPC says stop it when intending to become pregnant.

    2
    Unplanned pregnancy

    Do not stop suddenly without advice. Arrange a specialist review as soon as possible.

    3
    During pregnancy

    Agree and document the plan with the person, the ADHD specialist and the maternity team (perinatal mental health where needed). Extra growth scans may be offered.

    4
    Around birth

    Use in the weeks before delivery can cause short-term withdrawal signs in the baby (jitteriness, poor sleep, breathing or feeding problems). Tell the neonatal team.

    Epilepsy, surgery and older age

    Four situations where the usual plan needs adjusting. Choose one.

    Epilepsy

    ADHD is more common in people with epilepsy. Epilepsy is not a reason to withhold treatment, but titrate more slowly and monitor more often.

    What the SmPCs say
    • Methylphenidate: use with caution; stop if seizures become more frequent or start for the first time
    • Lisdexamfetamine: stop with new or worse seizures
    • Atomoxetine: consider stopping if a seizure occurs; caution with drugs that lower the seizure threshold
    Interactions
    • Methylphenidate may raise phenytoin, phenobarbital and primidone levels
    • Guanfacine may raise valproate levels
    • Carbamazepine and phenytoin (inducers) lower guanfacine levels
    Ask this

    “Have your seizures changed in how often they happen, or how they look, since starting?”

    What to do

    New or worse seizures: review the ADHD medicine and stop any that might contribute. After investigation, reintroduce cautiously if it is unlikely to be the cause. Work with the epilepsy team.

    Surgery

    Halogenated anaesthetic gases (for example sevoflurane) plus a stimulant can cause a sudden rise in BP during surgery. Guanfacine has the opposite risk: low BP, and rebound if stopped suddenly.

    What the SmPCs say
    • Methylphenidate: if surgery is planned, do not use it on the day of surgery
    • Dexamfetamine: the same advice
    • Lisdexamfetamine: no statement in the Elvanse SmPC
    Guanfacine
    • Do not stop it suddenly before an operation: rebound high BP
    • Adds to the BP-lowering effect of anaesthetics and other sedatives
    Ask this

    “Does the anaesthetist know which ADHD medicine you take, and when your last dose was?”

    What to do

    Agree a written peri-operative plan with the anaesthetist and the surgical team. For lisdexamfetamine and atomoxetine, follow local pre-operative policy (Clinical practice).

    Older adults

    Most licences do not cover older adults, and heart and blood-vessel risk rises with age. Treatment is a specialist, often off-label decision.

    What the SmPCs say (4.2)
    • Most methylphenidate brands: should not be used in the elderly (not studied over 65)
    • Lisdexamfetamine: limited data; thorough pre-treatment evaluation and ongoing BP and cardiovascular monitoring; dose adjustment may be needed
    • Atomoxetine: not systematically evaluated over 65
    • Guanfacine: should not be used in adults or the elderly
    Extra risks
    • Hypertension, coronary and cerebrovascular disease
    • More medicines, so more interactions
    • New attention problems may be cognitive decline, not ADHD
    Ask this

    “Have these problems been there all your life, or are they new?”

    What to do

    Careful cardiovascular assessment, low and slow titration, regular BP checks, documented off-label consent.

    Liver and kidneys

    Some ADHD medicines need lower doses when the liver or kidneys work less well, because the body clears them more slowly.

    Liver
    • Atomoxetine: moderate impairment (Child–Pugh B) halve the starting and target doses; severe (Child–Pugh C) use a quarter
    • Guanfacine: dose reduction may be needed
    • Lisdexamfetamine: not studied
    Kidneys
    • Lisdexamfetamine: severe renal impairment, maximum 50 mg a day
    • Guanfacine: dose reduction may be needed in severe impairment (GFR 15–29 ml/min)
    Ask this

    “Any liver or kidney problems, or recent blood tests?”

    What to do

    Check the current SmPC section 4.2 and the BNF before starting, and titrate slowly. Atomoxetine: stop for good if liver injury develops.

    Driving and travel

    Untreated ADHD can make driving less safe, and medication may improve it. Two separate laws apply: the duty to tell the DVLA about a medical condition, and the drug-driving law.

    The three driving rules

    1. Tell the DVLA if it affects driving

    People must tell the DVLA if their ADHD or its medicine affects their ability to drive safely; if it does not, they need not. Car or motorcycle: online or form A1. Bus, coach or lorry: form A1V. Not telling: fine up to £1,000.

    2. Amfetamines are “specified drugs”

    In England, Scotland and Wales it is an offence to drive above set limits of specified drugs, including amfetamines (dexamfetamine; lisdexamfetamine becomes dexamfetamine). Medical defence: prescribed, taken as advised, and not impairing driving. Methylphenidate is not a specified drug.

    3. Never drive impaired

    Driving while impaired by any drug is an offence everywhere in the UK. Stimulants can cause dizziness, drowsiness and blurred vision; guanfacine may have a moderate to severe effect on driving.

    Practical advice. Do not drive until you know how the medicine affects you, especially after starting or a dose change. Carry the prescription or keep the medicine in its labelled box. Drug-driving penalties include a minimum 1-year ban, an unlimited fine, up to 6 months in prison and a criminal record (GOV.UK). The specified-drug law does not cover Northern Ireland, but the impairment offence does.

    Travelling with a controlled drug

    • Hand luggagemedicine containing a controlled drug must be carried in hand luggage when entering or leaving the UK
    • Proof it is theirsa prescription or a prescriber’s letter: name, medicines, amounts, doses and strengths; original labelled packs
    • Check the destinationrules differ, and some countries restrict or ban amfetamines: check with that country’s embassy before travelling
    • 3-month limitmore than 3 months’ supply needs a Home Office personal licence, only in exceptional circumstances

    GOV.UK: Take medicine in or out of the UK; Travelling with controlled drugs (checked Oct 2026). Time zones: agree a dosing plan in advance so doses are not taken late at night (Clinical practice).

    How to explain it to a patient

    “You can drive on this medicine if you take it as prescribed and it doesn’t make you drowsy or dizzy. If your ADHD or the medicine ever affects your driving, you must tell the DVLA. When you fly, keep it in your hand luggage with your prescription.”

    Poor focus, restlessness and acting on impulse are not unique to ADHD. Many conditions can cause them, and many travel with ADHD. This module teaches you to tell them apart from the history, and what to treat first when ADHD comes with something else.

    Mimic, co-traveller or both?

    A differential diagnosis is the list of conditions that could explain the same symptoms. A comorbidity (coexisting condition) is a second condition present at the same time. Poor focus, restlessness and impulsivity are non-specific: low mood, worry, poor sleep, heavy drinking and many other conditions cause them too.

    Key idea

    For every alternative, ask two questions. Could it explain the symptoms instead? Then it is a mimic. Could it be there as well? Then it is a co-traveller. Often the answer to both is yes.

    Three possible answers

    A mimic explains it

    The symptoms began with the other condition and go when it is treated. Do not diagnose ADHD now: treat that, then reassess.

    ADHD plus another condition

    ADHD was there from childhood, and the other condition meets its own criteria. Diagnose both, then decide what comes first (lesson 8).

    ADHD alone

    Nothing else explains the picture better. Record what you considered and why you ruled it out.

    This is criterion E of DSM-5-TR: the symptoms are not better explained by another mental disorder and do not occur only during a psychotic disorder. ICD-11 adds “not due to a substance or medication”. NICE asks for coexisting conditions to be assessed in every diagnosis.

    Comorbidity is the rule

    28%of autistic people also have ADHD
    23%of adults in treatment for substance use have ADHD
    17%of adults with bipolar disorder have ADHD (8% the other way round)

    So finding one condition never ends the search.

    Two ways to get it wrong

    Missed ADHD

    ADHD is labelled only as anxiety, depression or a personality disorder; NICE warns this is more likely in girls and women. In autism or intellectual disability, new problems are put down to the known condition: this is called diagnostic overshadowing.

    ADHD that is not ADHD

    A mimic is labelled ADHD: sleep apnoea, a head injury, daily cannabis, a mood episode or a new medicine. The person gets a stimulant and the real problem is missed.

    1. Behavioural: ODD and conduct disorder. These are common in children with ADHD, and NICE lists them among groups with increased ADHD prevalence. Offer parents a parent-training programme; persistent aggression may need specialist (tertiary) advice before antipsychotics are considered.
    2. Neurodevelopmental: autism, tics, learning difficulties. These frequently coexist.     3. Mood and anxiety: anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder. These can mimic ADHD (poor concentration, restlessness) or coexist with it. ADHD is trait-like from childhood, while mood disorders are episodic. Stop ADHD medication during acute mania or psychosis.

    Mood, psychosis and eating

    Mood problems cause wrong answers in both directions: ADHD missed as depression, and depression called ADHD. The key is time. Mood disorders come in episodes, a change from the person’s usual self. ADHD is a trait, how the person has always been.

    Key idea

    Ask about episodes, not symptoms. Almost everyone with ADHD says yes to “distractible” and “racing thoughts”. What matters is whether these came as a spell that was different from normal.

    Emotional outbursts: ADHD, EUPD or a mood episode?How long it lasts and what sets it off
    ADHD emotional dysregulation

    Fast and intense, settles within minutes to hours. Set off by frustration, waiting or criticism. Lifelong.

    Emotionally unstable personality disorder

    Hours, sometimes days. Set off by rejection, abandonment or shame, with emptiness and self-harm.

    Mood episode

    Most of the day, nearly every day: 2 weeks or more for depression, 4 days or more for hypomania. Often no clear trigger.

    The trigger and the content of the emotion separate them better than the clock alone. Based on DSM-5-TR, ICD-11 and Kooij et al. 2019.

    Anxiety, trauma and personality

    Worry, trauma and an emotionally unstable personality all bring restlessness, poor focus and impulsive acts. To tell them apart, ask what drives the symptom, and what happens just before it.

    Key idea

    In ADHD, attention drifts to whatever is more interesting. In anxiety and trauma it is pulled to a worry, a memory or a threat. In EUPD, impulsive acts follow rejection or shame.

    Trauma-informed, not trauma-focused. You need the timeline (before or after?), not the details. Do not press for an account of the trauma in an ADHD assessment; think about safeguarding. Looked-after children and people in the justice system have more trauma and more ADHD.

    Substances, medicines and sleep

    These mimics change from day to day: they follow what the person takes and how they sleep. So the best test is a natural experiment: what is the person like when sober, off the medicine, or after good sleep?

    Neurodevelopmental overlaps

    Neurodevelopmental conditions are lifelong too, so the course does not separate them from ADHD: the pattern does. They often travel together. NICE lists autism, tic disorders, learning disability and specific learning difficulties among groups with more ADHD.

    What to treat first

    When ADHD comes with something else, ask two more questions: what must come first, and what can run in parallel? NICE asks for one holistic, shared treatment plan that weighs the relative impact of each condition.

    Three rules that change the plan

    3Same medicines, slower

    With anxiety, tic disorders or autism, offer the same medicine choices as anyone else. Titrate more slowly and monitor more often with any condition below.

    Recap and practise

    5 things to remember

    1. Two questions every time. Could it explain the symptoms instead (a mimic)? Could it be there as well (a co-traveller)? Often both.

    Four settings, four jobs

    Community pharmacist

    Main job: checks every controlled drug prescription, counsels, keeps the CD register and sees patterns over months.

    Watch for: early requests, a changed brand, cold remedies bought over the counter.

    Hospital pharmacist

    Main job: medicines reconciliation (an accurate list of every medicine) within 24 hours of admission, patients’ own CDs on the ward, and advice before surgery.

    Watch for: missed doses on the ward; stimulants still given during psychosis or mania.

    GP practice pharmacist

    Main job: continues prescribing and monitoring under the shared care protocol, if it is within their competence.

    Watch for: overdue checks or annual review; requests to change a dose outside the protocol.

    Specialist-team pharmacist

    Main job: titration clinics, medication and annual reviews, advice to primary care; with training, assessment.

    Watch for: complex cases, off-label use, and supporting less experienced colleagues.

    Training, not job title, decides who can do what. NICE never says “doctor”. It asks for a professional with training and expertise in ADHD to diagnose, to start medication and to review it each year. A pharmacist can do these jobs when trained, working within a specialist team’s governance, and within their own competence (lesson 8).

    Counselling that works for ADHD

    ADHD affects exactly the skills a person needs to manage a medicine: remembering to order it, collect it and take it on time. So the best counselling is short, in the same order every time, written down and checked back. Follow the five steps in the picture.

    Make it ADHD-friendly

    • One thing at a timeShort sentences in the same order each visit; pause and check after each part.
    • Write it downInstructions in words or pictures that stay with the medicine.
    • Build a routineSame point each day, such as after brushing teeth; parents oversee children’s doses.
    • Clear up myths“It won’t change who you are.” Check for misconceptions that stop people taking it.
    How to explain it to a patient

    “This is a controlled medicine, so a few rules. Take one each morning, swallowed whole. Keep it where only you can get to it, and never give any away. Order the next prescription a week before you run out, because we can’t lend you any. Can you tell me back how you’ll take it?”

    Checking a Schedule 2 prescription

    Methylphenidate, lisdexamfetamine and dexamfetamine are Schedule 2 controlled drugs (CDs): the group with the strictest prescription, storage and record rules. You may only supply one against a prescription that is complete, genuine and in date (Misuse of Drugs Regulations 2001, regulations 15 and 16). Atomoxetine and guanfacine are ordinary prescription-only medicines. Check a CD prescription in the same four steps every time; handing it over is in lesson 4.

    1
    Genuine, on the right form

    An NHS form (paper or electronic), or the private CD form with the prescriber’s ID number (England FP10PCD; Wales WP10PCD or WP10PCDSS). A UK prescriber address; a signature you know or have checked.

    2
    Every detail present

    Patient’s name and address; the dose (“as directed” is not a dose); the form; the strength; the total quantity in words and figures; signed and dated.

    3
    In date

    Supply from the appropriate date (the date signed, or a later start date the prescriber wrote) and no more than 28 days after it. Schedule 2 drugs cannot be on repeatable (repeat dispensing) prescriptions.

    4
    Clinically right

    Same product as before? A sensible dose? No more than 30 days’ supply, or a reason recorded: more is legal, so query it rather than refuse. Any new interactions, contraindications or odd collection pattern?

    Found a problem?
    You can fix itTwo kinds of error only

    Minor spelling or typing errors, or a total quantity in words or figures but not both, when the prescriber’s intention is clear. Amend it in ink, and sign and date it with your GPhC number.

    You cannot fix itAsk for a new prescription

    Anything else: a missing dose or strength, the wrong form, no prescriber number, or out of date. Contact the prescriber before the person runs out.

    !
    No emergency supply of a Schedule 2 CD

    Not at the person’s request, not at a prescriber’s request, and not as a “loan”. Help the person reach a prescriber the same day, for example through NHS 111 or the out-of-hours service.

    Private and online prescriptionsTwo extra checks when the prescriber is not NHS

    The form and the number. In a community pharmacy, a private Schedule 2 prescription must be on the private CD form with the prescriber’s identification number. Send the original each month to the NHS pricing body: in England, NHSBSA with form FP34PCD; in Wales, by the route Community Pharmacy Wales describes.

    Remote and online prescribers. The GPhC lists stimulants as “medicines liable to misuse” that need extra safeguards: the prescriber should not rely on a questionnaire alone, but verify what the person says, for example with their GP (GPhC, February 2025). If a supply looks unsafe, such as no GP details or prescriptions from several sources, contact the prescriber before you supply.

    Controlled drugs in the pharmacy

    Every box of an ADHD stimulant has a paper trail, from the moment it arrives to the moment it leaves. The rules come from the Misuse of Drugs Regulations 2001, the Safe Custody Regulations 1973 and NICE’s controlled drugs guideline. Follow one box through the pharmacy.

    Four numbers to know

    28 daysa Schedule 2 prescription is valid, and the last day to supply an owing
    30 daysthe most a CD prescription should cover, unless a reason is recorded
    2 yearsto keep the CD register, from its last entry
    48 hoursto report a CD incident to the accountable officer, ideally
    What goes in the CD cabinet?Tablets and capsules, liquids, and the non-stimulants

    Tablets and capsules of the three stimulants: always in the locked cabinet.

    Oral liquids containing an amfetamine or methylphenidate, such as dexamfetamine 1 mg/mL oral solution, are listed as exempt from the safe custody rules (Safe Custody Regulations, Schedule 1). They are still Schedule 2 for prescriptions, the register and supply, and most SOPs lock them away anyway.

    Atomoxetine and guanfacine are not controlled drugs: normal storage and no register.

    Who oversees controlled drugs?Accountable officers, local intelligence networks and SOPs

    Each designated body, such as an NHS trust, a health board or an NHS England region, has a controlled drugs accountable officer (CDAO). The CDAO makes sure safe systems are in place, receives concerns and incidents, and appoints the authorised witnesses who supervise destruction. CDAOs share intelligence through local intelligence networks (CD LINs). The rules are the Controlled Drugs (Supervision of Management and Use) Regulations 2013 in England and the equivalent 2008 regulations in Wales.

    Every pharmacy must have written SOPs for CDs: ordering, storage, access, supply, destruction and record keeping.

    On the wardHospital and mental health inpatient units

    A person’s own stimulants brought into hospital are still Schedule 2 CDs: store and record them under the ward’s CD policy. Reconcile all their medicines within 24 hours of admission, so doses are not missed. Flag ADHD medicine before surgery (Module 7), and remember that NICE says stop ADHD medicine during an acute psychotic or manic episode.

    Shortages and brand changes

    When the prescribed product is not available, the pharmacist cannot just hand over a different one. The prescription names the product, and for a Schedule 2 CD any change needs a new prescription. Work through it in four steps.

    Why there is no shortcut. A Serious Shortage Protocol (SSP) lets pharmacists supply an alternative without a new prescription. For Schedule 2 CDs this is possible only in very restricted circumstances, and in January 2025 the government said it had no plans to issue one for ADHD medicines. So a brand change means a new prescription.

    Where supply stands. In December 2025 the government reported that most ADHD medicine shortages had resolved; Equasym XL 30 mg was still short at that point (DHSC written answer, 2 Dec 2025). Shortages come and go, so check before every switch.

    Diversion: what the pharmacy sees

    Diversion means a medicine going to someone it was not prescribed for: shared with a friend before exams, sold, or taken to lose weight. NICE asks professionals to watch for it as people’s circumstances change. The pharmacy sees patterns no one else sees: when supplies are collected, who collects them, and where else prescriptions come from.

    Signs at the counter

    • Early or irregular collectionsRunning out before the expected date, again and again.
    • Lost, stolen or “spilled” suppliesRepeated stories, especially around exams.
    • More than one prescriberAn NHS and a private prescription at the same time, or a new prescriber with no history.
    • Prescription problemsAltered quantities, a photocopy, an unfamiliar prescriber or signature.
    • Who collectsA different collector each time, or someone unwilling to show ID.
    • What people sayFriends or housemates asking for tablets; requests for immediate-release or a higher strength.

    Clinical practice: no single sign proves diversion. A pattern is a reason to ask, not to accuse.

    What to do

    1
    Check the facts

    Your records: last supply dates and quantities, other prescribers. If a prescription may be forged, check with the prescriber before supplying.

    2
    Talk, without judging

    Ask what is happening. Advise on storage (a lockable box, away from others) and that the medicine is only for them.

    3
    Tell the prescriber

    Share the facts and your concern, ideally with the person’s knowledge. It is shared for their safe care.

    4
    Record and report

    Note what you saw and did. Report CD concerns to the CD accountable officer, ideally within 48 hours; suspected forgery or theft also to the police, as your SOP says.

    Is today’s supply safe?
    YesSupply and follow up

    The prescription is valid and genuine: supply as written, and share your concern with the prescriber.

    Not sureHold and phone

    If you doubt it is genuine or safe, contact the prescriber before you supply. This is your professional judgement.

    !
    Never lend or supply ahead of a prescription

    No “loans” until the prescription arrives and no emergency supply: both would be supplying a Schedule 2 CD without legal authority.

    Escalating concerns

    Most problems a pharmacist hears about can wait for the next review. A few cannot. Decide how fast, then who, then write it down. Module 7 explains what each symptom means; this lesson is about the route.

    Now

    999 or A&E

    • Chest pain now, collapse or a seizure
    • An overdose, including a child who took someone else’s tablets
    • A painful erection lasting hours
    • Someone at immediate risk of harming themselves

    How: call 999 or send to A&E; tell the prescriber afterwards.

    Same day

    Speak to a prescriber

    • New hallucinations, mania or suicidal thoughts
    • Yellow skin or eyes, or dark urine, on atomoxetine
    • Fainting or palpitations on exercise, now settled
    • Several missed doses of guanfacine

    How: phone the prescriber or specialist team; out of hours, NHS 111 or the crisis line.

    Before the next supply

    Contact the prescriber

    • Weight loss or growth worries
    • New tics that bother the person
    • Early requests or other diversion signs
    • A new interacting medicine, or a pregnancy

    How: phone, secure email or a written note; agree who will act.

    Routine

    Advise, record, review

    • Smaller appetite at lunch
    • Taking a little longer to fall asleep
    • A mild headache in the first week

    How: practical advice, a note in the record, a check at the next supply; report suspected side effects on the MHRA Yellow Card scheme.

    Consent, confidentiality and safeguardingWhen you can share, and when safety comes first

    Sharing a concern with the prescriber for the person’s safe care is part of your job, and best done with their knowledge (GPhC standards 2 and 7). If a child or an adult at risk may be harmed, for example a parent taking a child’s stimulant, follow your safeguarding policy: safety comes before confidentiality. Always record what you found, who you told and when.

    How to explain it to a patient

    “I’m not stopping your medicine, but I’d like to speak to your prescriber today about the dizziness you described. Is that OK? I’ll ring them now and let you know what they say.”

    Governance, supervision and leading a service

    A clinical lead is responsible for the system, not only their own patients: the rules everyone follows, who may do what, how you know care is safe, and what happens when something goes wrong. That system is called clinical governance. It has five parts.

    1
    Part 1 of 5

    Protocols and scope

    Write down how the service works and who may do what.

    • Shared care protocolAgreed with primary care, with clear lines back to the team: NICE NG87 1.1.2.
    • Titration and review protocolsDose steps, contact schedule, thresholds and who to call.
    • Scope documentsEach prescriber’s agreed scope, reviewed at least yearly (GPhC).

    Good signAnyone in the team can find the current protocol and knows which version is in use.

    Warning sign“We all just know how it works”: nothing written, nothing to audit against.

    2
    Part 2 of 5

    Supervision

    Everyone who prescribes or reviews has someone to talk to and someone who checks their work.

    • Clinical supervisionRegular case discussion, including incidents and near misses.
    • Record reviewSample notes and prescriptions against the protocol; give feedback.
    • Growing new prescribersAs a Designated Prescribing Practitioner, use the Royal College of Pharmacy’s DPP framework; agree scope for newly qualified prescribers.

    Good signSupervision records show cases discussed and scope extended step by step.

    Warning signA new prescriber asked to titrate alone because the waiting list is long.

    3
    Part 3 of 5

    Audit and data

    Measure care against a standard, change something, then measure again.

    • StandardsNG87 monitoring and annual review; NG46 30-day supplies; MHRA brand prescribing.
    • Prescribing dataLook for trends and very high or rising volumes.
    • Close the loopShare the result, change one thing, and re-audit.

    Good signA re-audit shows the change worked, and the team knows why.

    Warning signAn audit is done once, filed, and never repeated.

    4
    Part 4 of 5

    Safety and controlled drugs

    Make it easy to report, and act on what you learn.

    • IncidentsReport through your organisation’s system; CD incidents to the CDAO, ideally within 48 hours.
    • AlertsAct on national patient safety alerts within the set time.
    • Side effectsYellow Card reports, and learning shared across the team.

    Good signNear misses are reported as often as harm, and the team hears what changed.

    Warning signStaff fear blame, so CD register errors are “fixed” quietly.

    Part 5 of 5

    Service design

    Shape the service around NICE and the people who use it.

    • Specialist teamsMultidisciplinary teams for children and for adults, with shared care protocols: NICE NG87 1.1.2.
    • TransitionReassess at school-leaving age and plan the move to adult services, usually by 18.
    • TrainingSpecialist teams train primary care and other professionals.

    Good signRemote clinics build in ID checks, GP contact and record access for stimulants (GPhC, 2025).

    Recap and practise

    5 things to remember

    1. Every supply is a legal check: complete, genuine and within 28 days, and never an emergency supply or a “loan” of a Schedule 2 CD.
    2. Counsel for ADHD: short, in the same order, written down and checked back; plan the next supply before this one runs out.
    3. Never swap at the counter: a brand change needs a new prescription; check SPS first and owe within 28 days.
    4. Act on concerns at the right speed: 999, same day, before the next supply, or routine; check, talk, tell the prescriber and record.
    5. Competence sets scope: NICE asks for training and expertise, not a job title; leads build the protocols, supervision and audit that keep care safe.

    1

    2

    3

    4

    Every product at a glance

    Four things that differ between brands

    SwallowingTablet or beads?

    Concerta XL-type tablets: swallow whole; never chew, divide or crush. Bead capsules (Ritalin XL-type, Medikinet XL, Equasym XL) can be opened onto a spoonful of soft food. Elvanse can be mixed into yoghurt, water or orange juice.

    In the stoolAn empty shell is normal

    Osmotic tablets (Concerta XL, Affenid XL, Atenza XL, Matoride XL) can leave an empty tablet shell in the stool. It is harmless: the medicine has been released. The Xaggitin XL and Xenidate XL SmPCs describe no shell.

    Switching brands, formulations and medicines

    Switches happen in shortages, for side effects, when the shape of the day changes, or at 18. Module 5 explains why products differ. Here is the decision aid: ask four questions in order and stop at the first one that applies.

    Words used here: MR = modified-release (long-acting); IR = immediate-release (short-acting). Bioequivalent = the same dose gives the same blood levels over time. SPS = the NHS Specialist Pharmacy Service, which publishes the bioequivalence groups.

    1
    Same SPS group?

    Tablet group (Concerta XL, Affenid XL, Atenza XL, Delmosart, Matoride XL, Xaggitin XL, Xenidate XL) or capsule group (Ritalin XL, Meflynate XL, Metyrol XL, Focusim XL): switch at the same dose and quantity.

    2
    Medikinet XL or Equasym XL?

    Medikinet XL: possible, with particular care (not fully bioequivalent). Equasym XL: no bioequivalent product; do not switch it or prescribe it generically.

    3
    A different shape?

    Tablet to capsule, or MR to IR: no like-for-like conversion. Use an SmPC table only where one exists; otherwise the specialist sets the new dose.

    4
    A different medicine?

    There is no conversion factor. Start the new medicine at its own starting dose and titrate, in the NICE order.

    Overdose and serotonin toxicity

    An overdose of an ADHD medicine is an emergency, even if the person looks well, because long-acting products can cause late effects. Serotonin toxicity (serotonin syndrome) means too much serotonin activity, usually when two serotonergic medicines are taken together. Your job is to recognise both and get the person to the right care quickly.

    1
    Get help now

    Call 999 or go to A&E, and take the pack. Do not try to make the person sick and do not give food or drink. Not sure it is harmful? NHS 111.

    2
    Pass on the facts

    The product and brand (MR or IR matters), the strength, how many, when, and anything else taken, including alcohol.

    Serotonin toxicity: four groups of signs

    It has been reported when methylphenidate, lisdexamfetamine or atomoxetine is taken with serotonergic medicines such as SSRIs and SNRIs. Symptoms usually start within hours of a new serotonergic drug, a dose rise or an overdose.

    Mental stateAgitated and confused

    Agitation, restlessness, confusion, hallucinations; coma when severe.

    AutonomicHot, fast and sweaty

    Fast pulse, unstable blood pressure, high temperature, sweating and flushing.

    Neuromuscular: the key signClonus

    Rhythmic jerking (often at the ankles, or eyes flicking side to side), brisk reflexes, tremor, poor coordination and stiffness, worse in the legs.

    GutSick and loose

    Nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea.

    Act the same day. Suspected serotonin toxicity needs urgent medical assessment; a high temperature or muscle rigidity is an emergency (999). Methylphenidate must be stopped as soon as possible if it is suspected.
    The Hunter criteriaHow clinicians confirm serotonin toxicity

    In someone taking a serotonergic drug, serotonin toxicity is likely if any one of these is present (Dunkley and colleagues, 2003):

    1. Spontaneous clonus
    2. Inducible clonus, plus agitation or sweating
    3. Ocular clonus, plus agitation or sweating
    4. Tremor plus brisk reflexes (hyperreflexia)
    5. Muscle rigidity (hypertonia), plus a temperature above 38°C, plus ocular or inducible clonus

    Neuroleptic malignant syndrome, the main mimic, follows antipsychotics, develops over days and causes slow, “lead-pipe” rigidity rather than clonus (Boyer and Shannon, 2005).

    Recap and practise

    5 things to remember

    1. Look up the brand, not just the drug. Release, food rules, whether it can be opened, excipients and the adult licence all differ by product.
    2. Swap like for like only within an SPS group. Medikinet XL needs particular care, Equasym XL never; any other switch is a specialist decision with a new prescription.
    3. Missed doses: quote the SmPC where it has a rule (Ritalin-type: never double; Elvanse: next day; Intuniv: two or more missed, re-titrate) and call everything else judgement.
    4. At 18, check the licence: Equasym XL, Matoride XL, Medikinet tablets, dexamfetamine and guanfacine are off-label in adults. Start planning at school-leaving age, with the CPA from 16.
    5. Overdose: 999 or A&E now, even if well; MR methylphenidate and lisdexamfetamine act late, and lethargic children on guanfacine need up to 24 hours’ observation. Serotonin toxicity: clonus is the key sign; stop methylphenidate.
