1. Foundations of ADHD

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterised by persistent patterns of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that interfere with functioning. It involves dysregulation of dopamine and norepinephrine signalling, particularly in the prefrontal cortex (executive function, attention, impulse control).

Key points for pharmacists: Symptoms must begin before age 12, be pervasive across settings, and cause at least moderate impairment. Prevalence ~2.5% in adults. Under-recognised in females.

Presentations: Predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, or combined. Symptoms often evolve with age — hyperactivity may become internal restlessness in adults.

2. Assessment & Diagnosis (NICE NG87 1.3)

Diagnosis is made only by a specialist with expertise in ADHD. It requires:

  • Full clinical and psychosocial assessment (symptoms across domains and settings)
  • Full developmental and psychiatric history
  • Observer reports and mental state examination
  • Assessment of coexisting conditions, needs, and physical health
DSM-5 criteria (adults 17+): ≥5 symptoms of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity for ≥6 months, onset of several symptoms before age 12, present in ≥2 settings, clear functional impairment, not better explained by another disorder.

Rating scales (ASRS, Conners, DIVA-5) are adjuncts only — never sole basis for diagnosis. QbTest may support diagnosis in ages 6–17.

3. Differential Diagnosis

ADHD symptoms overlap with many conditions. Full assessment must exclude or identify co-occurrence of:

  • Anxiety and depression (common comorbidities; timeline helps distinguish)
  • Sleep disorders, substance use, autism spectrum disorder
  • Learning disability, thyroid dysfunction, medication effects
  • Personality disorders, bipolar, trauma-related presentations
Pharmacist tip: Always consider whether current medications (e.g. sedating antihistamines, antipsychotics) or substances could be contributing to attention difficulties.

4. Management Overview

Non-pharmacological first: Environmental modifications, ADHD-focused parent/carer training (especially children), structured support, elements of CBT for residual impairment. Medication is offered only when symptoms cause persistent significant impairment after these have been reviewed.

Pharmacological Hierarchy (NICE NG87)

  • Children ≥5 years: Methylphenidate first-line → if inadequate after 6-week trial → lisdexamfetamine → then atomoxetine or guanfacine.
  • Adults: Lisdexamfetamine or methylphenidate first-line → switch after 6-week adequate trial of the other → atomoxetine if both inadequate or not tolerated.
Modified-release preparations preferred for adherence, pharmacokinetics, and lower diversion risk.

5. Mechanisms of Action

All core ADHD medications enhance catecholamine signalling, primarily dopamine and norepinephrine, in key circuits.

  • Methylphenidate: Blocks dopamine and norepinephrine reuptake transporters.
  • Lisdexamfetamine: Prodrug converted to d-amphetamine; blocks reuptake and promotes release of monoamines. Lower abuse potential than immediate-release amphetamines.
  • Atomoxetine: Selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor. Increases NE (and DA via NET in PFC). Delayed onset (weeks), non-stimulant, useful when stimulants contraindicated or not tolerated.

6. Titration Protocols

Titration is specialist-led. Start low, increase against symptoms and adverse effects. Record symptoms and side effects at baseline and each change. Slower titration if comorbidities present. Aim for lowest effective dose.

Typical adult starting doses (BNF-aligned):
Methylphenidate XL: 18 mg daily
Lisdexamfetamine: 30 mg daily (or 20 mg if cautious start)
Atomoxetine: 40 mg daily

After 6 weeks at an adequate dose with insufficient response, switch to the alternative first-line agent before considering non-stimulants.

7. Monitoring (NICE NG87 1.8)

Baseline: mental health, substance risk, height/weight, pulse, blood pressure, cardiovascular history. ECG only if clinical indication.

  • Heart rate and blood pressure: before and after each dose change, then at least every 6 months.
  • Weight: every 6 months in adults; more frequently in children (every 3 months if ≤10 years).
  • Height: every 6 months in children and young people; plot on growth charts.
  • Annual specialist review of continued need, efficacy, and adverse effects.
  • Monitor for tics, sleep disturbance, mood changes, sexual dysfunction (atomoxetine), and diversion risk.
Action thresholds: Sustained resting tachycardia (>120 bpm), arrhythmia, or clinically significant BP rise → reduce dose and refer.

After dose stabilisation, prescribing and routine monitoring can transfer to primary care under shared care arrangements (NICE recommendation; not automatic — GP agreement required).

8. Case-Based Practice

Use the materials from your ADHD Drive folder (workbook, case studies, exam) for practice. Key skills: recognising when to refer, understanding titration decisions, interpreting monitoring results, and supporting shared care.

Example scenario prompts:
  • Adult with inadequate response to methylphenidate after 6 weeks at optimised dose — next step?
  • Child on stimulant with falling weight centile — management options?
  • Patient with history of substance misuse — preferred agent and precautions?
